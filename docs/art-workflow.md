# Deliberate pixel art with Pixelbolt

Pixelbolt supplies exact pixels and inexpensive revisions. The author still has to design the silhouette, choose meaningful color shapes and judge the motion.
## Write a brief outside the recipe

Keep an `asset.brief.md` beside the authoritative source. Record:

- Native dimensions and intended display scale. Review at 1× and the intended integer scale.
- Three silhouette landmarks and the negative spaces that distinguish the subject.
- Palette roles: outline, occlusion, shadow, local color, illuminated plane, accent. State light direction.
- Projection and depth: side view, 3/4 top-down, isometric 2:1 or top-down, the same for every asset in the set; the horizon or ground row; and the depth planes (backdrop, play plane, foreground) with how each reads at its distance. See [depth and perspective](#depth-and-perspective).
- Material rules: growth direction for plants, planes for stone, expansion/fragmentation for effects.
- Outline treatment and any deliberate isolated pixels or alpha blends.
- Action intent, contact/release/impact poses, timing, ground origin and attachments.
- Scene role: focal subject, navigable surface, background or foreground framing.
- Acceptance judgments, revisions, remaining weaknesses and source provenance.

Briefs and review notes are sidecars. Do not add these fields to a version-1 sprite recipe.

## Quality checkpoints

1. **Silhouette.** Author a connected contour with a few strong landmarks. Inspect `--view silhouette --native`; record whether identity survives without interior detail. Reshape an ambiguous outline before adding texture.
2. **Large color groups.** Use `--view grayscale` against the intended background. A connected shadow plane should describe a surface. Remove scattered texture that does not explain form.
3. **Materials.** Compare related assets. Leaves branch from stems; stone highlights follow planes; water flows and collects; crystal breaks along a fault and leaves debris. Palette swaps alone do not establish material behavior.
4. **Key poses.** Author contact, compression, passing and extension for locomotion; anticipation, release and recovery for an action. Replacing a drawn part is often more useful than translating the same stamp.
5. **Timing.** Inspect `--animation run --view onion --diagnostics`, then actually watch the studio or exported APNG. Sheets and timing strips cannot show perceived weight. Repeated poses/holds remain valid. The studio timeline uses expanded animation order, including repeated occurrences; pink is previous and cyan next. Once-only animations have no wrapped neighbor.
6. **Scene.** Use `preview room.scene.json` to check native size, value separation, density, overlap and reaction/aftermath. A good sprite can fail against its background. Keep a failed comparison when it teaches a specific correction. Where things pass in front of each other, render the [depth view](#depth-and-perspective) at those moments and read who covers whom.
7. **Revision.** Read the regional grid; make a bounded patch; inspect its changed-pixel report. Preserve unrelated poses deliberately. Keep an undo revision and recheck motion after the correction.

These are authoring decisions, not user permission gates. Passing validation, increasing frame count, or producing many variants does not pass an artistic checkpoint.

## Inspection options

The CLI and MCP `pixel_inspect` support `view: color|silhouette|grayscale|onion|tile`, `native`, `diagnostics`, `maxCells`, `step` and `offset` in addition to existing frame/animation/region/grid options. CLI spellings are `--view`, `--native`, `--diagnostics`, `--max-cells`, `--step`, `--offset`. `step`/`offset` keep every nth cell from position k before `maxCells` samples evenly; evenly spaced samples of a cycle with a repeating pattern (a strobe alternating A and B frames) can land on one phase or alternate between phases, so pick the phase with a stride. Frame names in `frames` accept `*` and `?` patterns. Silhouette uses one opaque light tone for every visible pixel, including translucent ones. Grayscale uses weighted RGB luma and retains alpha. Tile repeats each frame 3×3 and reports seam evidence (see [Scene and tile review](#scene-and-tile-review)); it repeats whole frames, so it takes no region. None modifies source pixels; grids always report original pixels.

With `--out review.png --native`, the CLI also writes `review.native.png`, checking both paths before writing. MCP returns an additional native-size PNG. Sampled sheets explicitly report total/shown/omitted and original sequence positions. Their cell durations are original holds, **not** a playable retiming of the sampled sequence. `pixel_render` automatically bounds large previews to at most 256 cells (fewer for large canvases), still exports every animation entry, and returns `playback` pointing to its HTML preview.

`--layers body,head` / `layers: ["body","head"]` isolates named layers through frame inheritance and transforms. It respects layer visibility/opacity and omits background, frame-level operations and final canvas corrections: those pixels have no layer identity. The original recipe is validated before isolating. `--reference earlier.json` / `reference: "revision-id"` adds a same-origin changed-pixel comparison; the CLI writes `review.reference.png` when a diff image exists and `--out` is supplied.

Diagnostics report duplicate images, empty frames, isolated visible pixels with no 8-connected neighbor, colors outside the declared palette, unusually large loop-boundary changes. Per-frame stats also count the pixels a `cleanup` patch change would alter: `corners` (doubled L-shaped steps in one-pixel lines) and `strays` (pixels whose eight neighbours share one other colour). These are counts, not findings: in finished, hand-drawn art most L-steps are deliberate highlight strokes or small rounded corners, and most specks are texture. Check them on rotated, rewritten, compiled or imported frames. Coordinates/bounds are provided, with bounded lists and omission counts. Loop warnings compare the boundary with the median nonzero adjacent change, using a minimum threshold of four pixels. These are advisory heuristics: exempt intentional holds, sparks, transparent effects, literal colors or cuts in the brief. They neither score beauty nor alter artwork. Animation timing includes expanded positions, original start times, durations and neighbor positions. Foot sliding needs authored contact points and scene movement; pixel differences alone cannot diagnose it.

## Regional corrections and scope

`pixel_patch`, CLI `patch`, and `patchRecipe` accept these canvas-coordinate changes:

```json
[
  {"grid":"frames[still]", "scope":"frame", "value":{"x":6,"y":3,"rows":["..hh", ".hgg", "~ggg"],"erase":"~"}},
  {"move":"frames[still]", "value":{"x":6,"y":3,"w":4,"h":3,"dx":1,"dy":0,"mask":["..xx",".xxx",".xxx"]}},
  {"recolor":"frames[still]", "value":{"x":7,"y":3,"w":4,"h":3,"from":"g","to":"h"}}
]
```

Another canvas change, `{"cleanup":"frames[still]", "value":{"corners":true, "strays":true}}`, proposes pixel-art cleanup as ordinary corrections, after Pixel Composer's De-Corner and De-Stray filters. It accepts the same optional region, `mask` and `scope`, plus `colors` to limit which original colours may change, and reports how many corners and strays it fixed. It is never applied on its own: preview it, read the changed pixels, and keep eyes, sparks and deliberate right angles.

Grid dots/spaces preserve pixels. Erasure needs an explicit non-palette character such as `~`; it may not be a dot or space. Masks match region size: `x` selects, `.` preserves. Move captures selected RGBA before clearing, so overlapping moves carry highlights and existing corrections. Selected transparent pixels also move and replace destination pixels. Every selected destination must fit; moves do not silently clip.

Regional batches have a 67,108,864 estimated-pixel work limit, including full-frame comparisons needed to preserve dependent poses. Split large batches or reduce scope when that budget is exceeded. Failures leave the source untouched.

The default scope is `inherited`, preserving existing propagation. `scope: "frame"` preserves other poses by recording compensating final-canvas corrections on affected dependents. The edit report lists those protected frames. These corrections intentionally pin those pixels, including their current color: later palette edits will not recolor literal compensations. Use earlier revisions to undo. To edit a named set of frames, submit one change per target with `scope: "frame"`; to alter all instances of a shared drawing, patch its symbol rows instead.

Named selections live in an overlay sidecar with `space: "canvas"`. They do not follow a moving body part. For part-local work, edit that part definition in a pose source and recompile.

## Rebuild-safe overlays

```sh
node bin/pixelbolt.js overlay base.json --changes edits.json --selections regions.json --out corrections.json
node bin/pixelbolt.js patch rebuilt-base.json --changes corrections.json --out approved.json --image difference.png
```

`regions.json` maps names to `{space:"canvas",x,y,w,h,mask?}`. A grid/move/recolor change can reference one with `selection:"fern-tip"`; its value supplies action-specific fields. `createOverlay` and `applyOverlay` offer the same JavaScript API.

An overlay carries a SHA-256 fingerprint of the whole base, canonicalized by object-key order but preserving array order. Rebuilding the same base reproduces the correction. Overlays made only of canvas changes (`paint`, `grid`, `move`, `recolor`) also record per-frame evidence: a fingerprint of every frame they target and of every frame inheriting from those, the frame each change addresses, and the palette colors the changes paint with. When the base changed elsewhere (another frame's duration, a new animation, a different pose), the overlay still applies and reports `rebased: true`. **Any change to a frame it touches, to the frame a path addresses, or to a palette color it uses is a conflict**, and nothing is applied. Overlays with structural `set`/`insert`/`remove` edits keep the whole-recipe rule. The tool never guesses that an old coordinate still identifies the same feature; after a conflict, inspect the new base and create a new overlay. Keep the generator, overlay and resulting recipe, and identify which file is authoritative. `examples/quality/fern-base.json` with the overlay `fern-correction.json` shows one.

## Authored poses, attachments and cues

Use a `pixelforge-poses` version-1 sidecar, described by [poses.schema.json](../poses.schema.json). It compiles into ordinary sprite recipes; it does not change their rendering semantics.

```json
{
  "format":"pixelforge-poses", "version":1, "name":"held-lamp", "width":16, "height":16,
  "palette":{"g":"#72967d","y":"#e7ce89"},
  "parts":{
    "arm":{"rows":["ggg","..g"],"anchor":[0,0],"points":{"grip":[2,1]}},
    "lamp":{"rows":["y","y"],"anchor":[0,0],"points":{"light":[0,1]}}
  },
  "poses":[{"name":"rest","duration":180,"origin":[4,5],"parts":[
    {"name":"forearm","part":"arm"},
    {"name":"held","part":"lamp","attach":{"part":"forearm","point":"grip"}}
  ],"markers":[{"name":"light","part":"held","point":"light"}]}]
}
```

`parts` define palette grids, local anchors and local named points. Each pose's `origin` is a source-canvas point (often the ground anchor). Instances draw in listed back-to-front order. Without `attach`, `at` offsets the instance's anchor from the pose origin. With `attach`, it offsets the anchor from a named point on any instance in the same pose (dependency cycles are rejected). Definition points use the part grid's upper-left origin. A pose can use a different part definition under the same instance name, such as an open versus curled hand. An instance with `"flipX": true` mirrors its grid, anchor and points inside the part. A pose written as `{ "name": "walk-l-1", "mirror": "walk-r-1" }` reflects an earlier pose around its origin, including parts, points and markers, and inherits its duration; it takes no parts, markers or origin of its own. The origin becomes the frame anchor, which names the corner between pixel columns originX − 1 and originX, and the mirror reflects around that corner: pixel column c becomes 2 × originX − 1 − c. That is the same reflection as the runtime's `drawFrame` with `flipX` and engines that flip around the atlas pivot, so a compiled left-facing pose and a runtime-flipped right-facing one draw identical pixels. Centre a symmetric front view on the origin corner (for example columns 12–27 around origin x 20) and the side views mirror around the same line. No rotation, smoothing or automatic in-betweens are imposed unless you ask for them.

An instance with `"rotate": 30` turns its part clockwise by whole degrees (−360 to 360) around the centre of the part's anchor pixel, after any flip, and its points turn with it, so a held item stays in the hand. The rotated grid is baked into the compiled recipe as an ordinary, editable symbol named `<part>-r<degrees>` (`-fr` when flipped). Rotation never invents colours: three Scale2x passes enlarge the grid eight times, which rounds staircase edges, and each output pixel samples the enlarged grid where its rotated centre lands (the RotSprite approach). Right angles are exact. A cleanup pass then removes doubled corners the resampling leaves; set `"cleanup": false` to keep the raw result (`<part>-r<degrees>-raw`). Small parts lose detail at odd angles, so inspect the symbol and correct it by patching the compiled recipe or drawing a replacement part for key poses.

A pose written as `{ "name": "swing-1", "tween": { "from": "raise", "to": "strike", "t": 0.33, "ease": "in" } }` is an in-between of two earlier authored or tweened poses with the same part instances in the same order and the same attachments. Its origin, each instance's `at` offset and each `rotate` interpolate by the eased `t` and round to whole pixels and degrees; part definitions, flips and cleanup switch at the eased halfway point. Easing presets are `linear` (default), `hold`, `in`, `out`, `inOut`, `overshoot` and `bounce`. A tween takes no parts or origin, does not inherit markers (add its own if it needs cues) and cannot start from a mirrored pose; mirror the finished tween instead. [examples/swing.poses.json](../examples/swing.poses.json) rotates one sword through an eased swing. In-betweens are a starting point, not a substitute for authored contact, anticipation and recovery poses.

Compiled recipes carry each pose origin as the frame anchor (one recipe-level `anchor` when every pose shares it), and each marker's position as a named frame point, so the atlas alone tells a game where the feet, hand or blade tip are. Marker names are unique within a pose.

```sh
node bin/pixelbolt.js compile actor.poses.json --out actor.json --metadata actor.meta.json
node bin/pixelbolt.js preview actor.poses.json
```

The metadata exports each pose origin, resolved part anchors/points, markers, and expanded animation timing. Coordinates are **unscaled source pixels**, positive y down. For a game using `sheet.scale`, scale attachment coordinates explicitly; atlas rectangles already use exported pixels. Cue times are original milliseconds; divide by a positive playback rate in the consumer. Markers occur on entry into a pose, including repeated entries, reverse/pingpong positions and the first entry of a new loop. Nonlooping final holds do not repeatedly fire markers. Pixelbolt exports cues; the game owns event dispatch, collision and damage. Keep the `.poses.json` authoritative and reapply approved overlays after compilation.

### Structural limbs and bodies

Choose exactly one of `rows`, `bone` or `mass` per part. A bone is redrawn at its world angle, with integer pixels and palette ramp bands. Keep heads, hands, armour accents and props as grids where their particular shape matters.

```json
{
  "ramps": [["s", "a", "b", "c"]],
  "light": 225,
  "parts": {
    "forearm": {
      "bone": {"length": 7, "width": [3, 2], "ramp": 0, "base": 2, "outline": "k", "caps": ["socket", "round"]},
      "points": {"wrist": "end"}
    },
    "torso": {
      "mass": {"size": [10, 12], "ramp": 0, "base": 2, "outline": "k"},
      "points": {"shoulder": [4, -3]}
    }
  }
}
```

This fragment assumes those one-character keys exist in `palette`. Ramps run dark to light, with 2–32 keys; `ramp` selects a ramp and required `base` selects its main colour. Optional `outline` is a palette key, `"selective"` (the ramp's darkest colour on the shadow side), or `null`/omitted for no outline. `light` is a clockwise world angle, 0–359, default 225° (upper left).

Bone `length` is 1–64 pixels. `width` has two values (start/end) or three (start/middle/end), each 1–16. `caps` has a start and end choice from `socket`, `round`, `flat`, `point`, default `["socket","round"]`. Thin spines use even stair steps and snap within 4° of clean slopes by default; `snap:false` keeps the requested direction. A bone anchors at its start; `"end"` names its rasterized endpoint. Numeric points are `[distanceAlong, distanceAcross]` relative to the start. A mass anchors at its centre and its numeric points are offsets from that centre; a `flat` mass anchors at the middle of its base row, so its points are offsets from there (negative y is up into the dome). Structural parts cannot set a separate `anchor` or disable cleanup. Each distinct bone or mass drawing is charged its nominal pixel cost, and one compile may spend at most 8,388,608; real figures use a few percent. Reuse angles and squash values, or use smaller masses, if a source reaches it.

Mass `size` is `[width,height]`, each 2–64. An instance's `squash` is 0.5–2 in steps of 0.05, default 1: height multiplies by squash and width divides by it. The rasterizer adjusts the discrete contour to keep opaque area, including the outline, within 10% of the rest mass. Exact analytic ellipse dimensions can shift by a pixel to preserve that area. `flat: true` draws the top half of the ellipse instead: a dome with a flat base, for slimes, mounds and blobs that sit on the ground. Its `size` is the dome's width (an odd width exactly, an even one a pixel wider, since a mass is symmetric about its anchor column) and its height in rows, base row included; an outline adds a row under the base. Squash and rotation keep the base row on the anchor, so a squashed dome spreads along the ground instead of lifting off it, and a turned dome turns about the middle of its base. Area is kept the same way (a dome only two rows tall falls short at squash 1.9 or more).

### Relative joints, targets and views

`angle` on an instance is relative to the parent's resolved world angle, or world-relative for a root. `rotate` retains its absolute meaning; do not combine them. Both accept whole degrees −360 through 360. Attachments may name any instance in the same pose, including one painted later; cycles are errors and paint order remains the listed order. An `at` offset stays in canvas axes. Relative-angle tweens use the shortest arc; legacy absolute rotations retain their previous interpolation. Squash and reach targets also interpolate, with final integer coordinates and 0.05 squash steps.

Put `reach:[x,y]` on the last bone of a two-bone chain to target a **canvas** point. The child must attach to a named `"end"` point of its parent with no offset; reach replaces the child's `angle`/`rotate`. `bend` is `"forward"` (default) or `"back"`. Chains cannot share a solved bone. The solver chooses the closest integer endpoint, then the requested bend, smallest change from rest angles and lower angles. Many angle pairs often land on the same pixel, so the rest angles decide between them: give paired legs the same rest angles, or their cycles will differ. Outer unreachable targets straighten; inner unreachable targets use the closest fold. Warnings report the actual miss; `metadata.poses[name].parts` includes joint endpoints, world angles and reach error.

Grid parts can supply up to 16 `views`, keyed by whole degrees 0–359. Each view has `rows`, a required `anchor`, and all the base part's named `points`. A view within 22.5° is used directly; otherwise the base drawing follows the existing grid rotation. This preserves a small hand or blade at key angles. Ordinary mirrors remain pixel-identical to runtime flipping. Add `relight:true` to a mirror pose to reflect its joints and redraw bones/masses under the unchanged world light. Joints, points and bone shapes are exact reflections; only a 2-pixel limb's second column stays on the shadow side of the fixed light.

### Motion templates

`rig` assigns roles to instances in one authored rest pose: `body` (a root mass, flat or not), optional `head`, `ground`, `rest`, `legs` and `arms` (arrays of two- or three-bone chains), and `tail` (up to sixteen instances). Chain children attach to a named `"end"` point with zero offset. In a three-bone leg, the first bone supplies the upper pose and the last two solve the foot target. Give each chain's final bone a named endpoint, such as `"points": {"end": "end"}`.

[examples/hero.poses.json](../examples/hero.poses.json) is a complete rigged side-view figure with all six templates; start from it. `motions` maps animation names to templates. For example, `"run": {"template": "run", "speed": 48, "stride": 10, "lift": 5, "bob": 1, "lean": 8, "frames": 8, "duration": 40}` expands into named poses, contact markers and a looping animation. All positions and angles are deterministic integer samples. Mass squash rounds to 0.05 steps.

| Template | Parameters and defaults | Frames |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `walk` | `speed:24`, `stride:10`, `lift:2`, `bob:1`, `lean:0`, `swapDepth:false`, `duration:80` | 8 by default; even 4–64 |
| `run` | `speed:48`, `stride:10`, `lift:4`, `bob:1`, `lean:8`, `swapDepth:false`, `duration:80` | 10 by default; even 6–64 |
| `idle` | `breath:0.05` or `squash` (0.05–0.5), `duration:200`; head moves at most one pixel, secondary parts sway | 4 by default; 2–16 (from 5, named `idle-0`, `idle-1`…) |
| `jump` | `crouch:0.8`, `stretch:1.15`, `apex:4`, `duration:80` | crouch, take-off, rise, apex, fall, land, recover |
| `attack` | `arm:0`, `windup:-130`, `strike:50`, `hold:2`, `duration:80` | anticipation, strike, follow-through, recover; strike repeats `hold` times |
| `hurt` | `recoil:-20`, `squash:0.85`, `duration:80` | recoil, recover |

Durations are milliseconds per displayed frame; speeds are horizontal pixels per second and can be negative. Stride is the foot's travel envelope. Contact duration is bounded by both the gait's stance fraction and the time that fits within that stride. Each planted target moves backward at `speed × elapsedTime`, rounded only at the final target, so rounding does not accumulate. Lift phases return the foot forward. Alternate leg pairs begin half a cycle apart, so the poses alternate while the draw order stays as authored: in a side view the near arm, leg and anything held stay in front of the body. For a front or back view, where the stepping limb comes nearer the camera, set `swapDepth: true` to exchange paired limb subtrees, held items included, in the second half-cycle. When a bob or lean would lift a hip beyond its planted leg, every pose of that motion sinks by the same 1–4 pixels, so feet stay planted and the bob keeps its shape. These are editable template decisions, not a runtime skeleton.

`squash` on an idle replaces `breath` with a grounded squash and stretch: over the loop the body stretches by up to that share and then squashes, along a sine, in 0.05 steps, and each generated pose moves the body (and what is attached to it) so the body's lowest drawn pixel, outline included, sits on `rig.ground`. The pose origin stays, so the frames share one anchor. It suits a body that sits on the ground; a rig with `legs` stands on them, so it is refused there, as is `squash` with `breath`. The head bob and tail sway move their instance after grounding, so the body can't also be `rig.head` or part of `rig.tail`. Give it a `flat` mass body for a flat base. From five frames the poses are numbered and the motion review's pose spread compares the quarter phases (rest, full stretch, rest, full squash). Name the rest pose `idle-0` to make it the loop's first frame, so the sheet holds only the loop. An authored pose that stands in for a generated pose this way is yours and is never moved, so its body must already stand on `rig.ground` (lowest drawn pixel, outline included); a stand-in that doesn't is refused with its path and the row it reaches. [examples/slime-idle.poses.json](../examples/slime-idle.poses.json) is the quick start's slime at 32 × 32 doing this: eight frames, `squash: 0.15`, 140 ms.

`pixelbolt compile character.poses.json --expand` prints a source with templates replaced by poses; `--out new.poses.json` saves it with overwrite protection. `expandPoses(source)` is the equivalent JavaScript API. An authored pose with a generated name replaces that entire generated pose. Keep its markers and contacts when they still apply. The generated `motionInfo` annotations record animation speed and meaningful `keyPoses`; keep them when editing expanded sources so compilation and motion review round-trip. Do not combine `motionInfo` with unexpanded `motions`. Expanded sources retain named layers and deduplicate identical symbol drawings in a deterministic order.

Templates are starting points: an unsuitable stride, body lean or limb length can still produce a reach warning or an advisory motion failure. Inspect contacts, motion strips and the silhouette, then adjust parameters or override the affected pose. In-betweens and held strike entries are not treated as distinct key poses for the pose-spread threshold.

### Reviewing motion

Declare the ground pixel row with `"rig": {"ground": 23}` and name planted markers on each pose with `"contacts": ["nearFoot"]`. Contact names must be unique and refer to that pose's markers. Mirrors inherit contacts; tweens declare their own. Contacts remain in pose metadata and each animation entry, while the compiled recipe stays ordinary version 1.

`pixelbolt inspect character.poses.json --motion run --speed 48 --out run-review.png` measures the full animation and saves native and enlarged strips. For a compiled recipe, pass `--metadata character.meta.json`; `--ground row` overrides its ground. The JavaScript API is `inspectMotion(renderProject(recipe), {animation: "run", metadata, speed: 48})`. MCP `pixel_inspect` accepts `{source, view: "motion", animation: "run", speed: 48}`, or `{revision, metadata, view: "motion", animation: "run", speed: 48}` using the metadata returned by `pixel_compile` with `metadata: true`. Revisions save recipes; they do not implicitly recover authoring metadata.

Slide is the farthest distance between world-space positions of one marker in one continuous contact phase. The review samples **displayed-frame onsets**, not the continuous travel during a held drawing. Phases join across loop boundaries; a foot planted for the entire cycle includes its position at the next loop's start. Positive ground error means floating and negative means sinking. Loop review compares each joint's shortest angular jump with the largest jump inside the cycle made by that joint or, for paired rig limbs, by its partner: legs run half a cycle apart, so one leg's swing-to-contact jump falls on the seam while its partner makes it mid-cycle. Pose spread counts changed RGBA pixels divided by the union of the two opaque masks; adjacent poses use a 20% advisory threshold, and named `contact-a`/`contact-b` poses use 30%. In a side view those two can share most pixels although the legs have traded places, so posed figures with joint angles also pass the contact pair when some joint turns at least 20°; drawings without joints are judged by pixels alone.

All measurements are advisory and leave art unchanged. Missing contacts, speed, ground or joint angles produce `null` checks rather than a passing result. The strip shows the trajectory on the left and playback cells on the right, with planted footprints in yellow. It samples at most 32 positions, reports omissions and bounds image size; the numeric review still uses every frame. Travel too wide for the image is reported explicitly.

The studio opens `.poses.json` directly, including from `pixelbolt preview character.poses.json`, and preserves that source when saving. Choose **Motion** for these same checks and strips, or **Show contacts** during playback. **Travel (px/s)** and **Ground row** override review settings; playback speed changes only preview timing. Export still produces ordinary recipe assets.

## Scene and tile review

Use a separate `pixelforge-scene` version-1 manifest ([scene.schema.json](../scene.schema.json)). `assets` maps ids to recipes (inline or file references) or `{recipe, normal?, emissive?}`. `instances` draws in array order. An instance has `asset`, `at:[x,y]`, optional local `anchor`, integer `scale`, and either `frame`, `animation` or a `tilemap`. The anchor is one `[x, y]` in asset pixels (default `[0, 0]`), or `"frame"` to place every drawn frame by its own atlas anchor (per-frame or recipe-level), so an animated placement follows anchors that change between poses, as `drawFrame` does in a game. Tilemaps take `[x, y]` only. `repeat:[columns,rows]` and `step:[dx,dy]` create tile arrangements. Optional `sequence:[{time,frame|animation}]` cues choose a new state and restart that animation; a nonlooping reaction holds its final pose. Times increase strictly and stay below scene duration. Optional `trajectory:[{time,at,ease?}]` starts at time 0, interpolates between authored positions and rounds to whole pixels; it holds the last position. Each key's `ease` shapes the segment that starts at it: `linear` (default), `hold` (stay, then jump at the next key), `in`, `out`, `inOut`, `overshoot` or `bounce`. This is an explicit preview path, not inferred movement or physics.

Placements can relate to each other:

- **Named cues.** A scene-level `cues` map names times, `{"strike": 1200}`, and any `sequence` or `trajectory` time may then say `"strike"`, `"strike+90"` or `"strike-20"`, so the moment a blow lands is written once and the smear, the breaking pot and the debris follow it.
- **Hiding.** `hidden: true` starts a placement hidden until its first frame or animation cue, and a cue `{"time": "strike+250", "hide": true}` hides it again, so a smear or a burst needs no empty frame.
- **Attachment.** `attach: {"instance": "hero", "point": "blade"}` places this placement at a named point of another placement's current frame (frame `points`, which compiled poses export from their markers), plus `at` as an offset. It is not drawn while that frame has no such point. It stands on the other placement's ground line, straight below the point, and is lifted up to the point, so with `sort: "ground"` a held sword sorts with its hero: in front of the barrel the hero stands in front of. List order decides between the two, since they share a line: list the sword after the hero to draw it over the hero, or list a shield on the far arm before the hero. Without a `point` it follows where the other placement stands, which stays on the ground while that placement is lifted: the place for a contact shadow. The target is any uniquely named placement that is not a tilemap or attached itself, listed anywhere, so a shadow can be listed (and drawn) before the actor that casts it.
- **Ground sorting.** Placements with `sort: "ground"` are drawn together, where the first of them is listed, ordered by their ground line: the y of their placement point, which is the anchor's position. With ground anchors on props and actors, a hero walking above a barrel is drawn behind it and below it in front, as a game draws them. A tilemap with `sort: "ground"` joins the group tile by tile, each tile standing on the bottom row of its frame, so a hero behind a row of walls is covered by them and one in front covers them. A wall's cap and the face a rule draws below it stand on their own rows. Draw anything taller than its footprint, such as a tree, a pillar or a lamp post, as one frame taller than the tile: it hangs down from its cell's top and stands on its own bottom row, so its canopy covers whoever walks behind its trunk. A canopy drawn as a tile of its own stands on its own row, so a hero standing between it and the trunk's base draws over it. To let each character mark the cell a trunk stands in, raise the map by the overhang (`"anchor": [0, 16]` for 32-pixel trees on 16-pixel tiles). Leave floors unsorted and list them first. A recipe layer takes the same `sort: "ground"`, ordering its operations by the row each stands on ([frames and layers](agent-guide.md#frames-and-layers)). The [depth view](#depth-and-perspective) shows each ground line and lists who covers whom.
- **Lift.** `lift` draws a placement that many pixels above where it stands, and where it stands still orders it, so a hero jumping in front of a barrel stays in front of it. Moving `at` up instead would sort the hero behind the barrel at the top of the jump. Give a height for something that hovers, or keys of height over time for a jump: `"lift": [{"time": "jump", "height": 0, "ease": "out"}, {"time": "jump+150", "height": 12, "ease": "in"}, {"time": "jump+300", "height": 0}]`. Keys hold before the first and after the last, and each key's `ease` shapes the change to the next; `out` up and `in` down make an arc. Heights are whole pixels from 0 to 256. A frame point rises with its placement, and so does whatever is attached to it, such as a held sword or a light. A placement attached without a point stays on the ground: attach the contact shadow that way. A light following a placement without a point is where that placement stands: on the ground under a jump, and on its holder's ground line for a held torch. Tilemaps take no lift.
- **Haze.** A scene's `haze` is the colour far planes fade toward, usually the sky's: `"haze": {"color": "#b8c8d8"}`. A placement or tilemap with `haze` from 0 to 1 stands that far into it: after lighting, each of its colours moves that much of the way to the haze colour. A colour fades as a whole, never pixel by pixel, so each colour of the placement becomes exactly one colour and keeps its place among the others, like a far palette an artist mixes, and the art gains no gradient or dither. Use about 0.3 for a middle plane and 0.6–0.8 for a far one. Haze `colors` (up to 64) are the only colours hazed placements may end on: each faded colour becomes the nearest of them or the haze colour. List the game's whole palette to stay inside it, or a short ramp toward the haze to turn far planes into silhouettes; nearby colours then merge, as distant details do. The reply's `haze` lists each hazed placement with the colours its palette keys became before lighting, which an asset entry's `palette` can take to make the same far copy as a fixed asset.
- **Camera and parallax.** A scene's `camera` is the scene point at the view's top-left corner, so placements may lie anywhere and the view pans across them: `"camera": [{"time": 0, "at": [0, 0], "ease": "inOut"}, {"time": 2000, "at": [64, 0]}]`, or a fixed `[x, y]`. Keys take cue names, hold before the first and after the last, and each key's `ease` shapes the move to the next. At `[0, 0]` everything is where it is written, so author the opening view first. A placement's or tilemap's `parallax` is how much of the camera's movement it follows: 1 is the scene (the default), about 0.5 a far plane, 0.25 farther, 0 fixed on screen (a sky, a sun), and above 1 a plane nearer than the scene (posts or leaves in front). `[x, y]` sets each axis, such as `[0.5, 0]` for hills that keep their height while the camera rises. Each plane rounds its own share of the camera to whole pixels, so slow planes step less often, as in a game. Attached placements move with their target and take no parallax of their own. A light moves with the scene, or with the plane of what it follows. Ground sorting compares scene positions, so keep each sorted group on one plane. Clipping at the view's edges is no warning with a camera, since most of the scene is off screen at any time. The reply gives the `camera` position at that time; pair parallax with `haze` so far planes are also paler.
- **Shade placements.** `shade: -1` draws no colours: wherever the placement's frame is opaque, the pixels already drawn beneath move one step down the ramps of the asset's recipe. One shadow frame then darkens stone, floor, moss or brick correctly, and an attached shadow follows an actor across them.
- **Cast shadows.** `shadow: true` on a placement or tilemap casts a shadow from every scene light that reaches it. Its frame is a cutout standing on its ground line, turned to face the light, and its shadow stops that light: the pixels it falls on lose exactly what that light gave them. A shadow therefore takes the light's colour, reach, flicker and keys; two shadows from one light are no darker where they overlap; and with `mode: "ramp"` it stays in the palette. It falls away from the light, longer the farther its caster stands from the light and the nearer the caster's top comes to the light's `height`; a light's `at` counts as the point on the ground under it. A sun's shadows are parallel: each pixel of height falls the length of its `sun` the same way, wherever the caster stands. A shadow falls on what is drawn before its caster, or before its sorted group, as a game draws shadows on the ground under everything that stands, so standing placements never shadow each other or themselves. A lifted placement's shadow leaves its feet, and a tilemap casts from the middle of each tile. A light that rides a placement, or something attached to it, casts no shadow of it, so a hero carrying a lantern casts shadows only from the other lights. A caster straight under a light, or under a sun at `[0, 0]`, casts nothing, so keep contact shadows under feet. A shadow shows only where its light showed: on lit placements (`scope: "all"` lights plain floors) and as far as the light changed a colour, so a low light with a short reach throws short, faint shadows and a floor colour at the dark end of its ramp has nowhere darker to go. The reply's `shadows` lists each caster (the first 16) with its light, the pixels its shadow covers, how far it reaches from where the caster stands and, for a single caster, the way it falls (`toward`); a name several placements share gets `#index`, as in the depth report.

```sh
node bin/pixelbolt.js preview examples/forest-spirit-lit.scene.json
node bin/pixelbolt.js scene examples/forest-spirit-lit.scene.json --out output/scene-review
```

The scene viewer offers playback, scrubbing, native size, grayscale, a pixel-density overlay, lighting controls and scene-settings download. Drag on the scene to move the chosen light (pressing on another light's marker picks that one); a dashed ring shows how far it reaches, and sliders set its position, height and radius, its colour and the scene's ambient light, all redrawn as you go. A keyed light's radius also reaches the keys that set none; when every key sets its own, its Radius slider is off. Changes are in memory until downloaded; the download is the scene as written with only the light fields you changed, so a scene opened with `preview` still names its asset and palette files. The export includes `scene.json`, lit/unlit stills, a browser viewer, aligned color/material atlases, and `alignment.json`. Serve its folder over HTTP for module loading, or use `preview` with the original manifest. Large rooms should be composed in a game: this review format is bounded to 256×256, 64 assets, 256 placement declarations, 4,096 expanded draws, 4,194,304 combined source-pass pixels, and 4,194,304 drawn pixels per view. Clipping warnings name the placements that overhang. It does not run a world state machine.

A scene `palette` re-themes every asset: a map of palette keys to colours, or a palette file the CLI and MCP resolve, applied to the keys each recipe already has (others are ignored), so one set of recipes renders as grey or blue stone from two small scene files. An asset entry `{"recipe": "floor.json", "palette": {...}}` adds overrides for that asset alone, after the scene's. Ramps name keys, so they follow the theme.

Assets can be inlined, or referenced so the manifest stays small: `"assets": { "lamp": "../recipes/lamp.json", "tower": { "recipe": "tower.json", "normal": "tower-normal.json" } }`. The CLI (`scene`, `preview`) resolves those files relative to the manifest, and their own palette references relative to each recipe; the MCP `pixel_scene` tool resolves them inside its root and also accepts `{"revision": id}`. The exported `scene.json` is the resolved, self-contained manifest.

A **tilemap** placement draws a whole character map as one declaration:

```json
{ "name": "ground", "asset": "shore", "at": [0, 0], "tilemap": {
  "rows": ["~~..", "~...", "~~.."],
  "legend": {
    "~": { "frames": ["water-a0", "water-b0"] },
    ".": { "animation": "shore-{mask}", "autotile": "blob", "match": ".g" }
  }
} }
```

`tile` defaults to the asset size. A legend entry names one `frame` or `animation`, or lists `frames`/`animations` variants that are picked deterministically by cell position, so repeated terrain does not tile visibly. With `autotile: "blob"` or `"cardinal"`, `{mask}` in the names becomes the cell's neighbour mask, computed from neighbours whose characters are in `match` (default: the same character). With `"corner"`, the frames of a corner set are drawn where four cells meet, half a cell up and left of a cell, so a map draws one more row and column of them; `{mask}` names which of the four cells around that point match, each point is drawn once for every legend entry among its cells, and the cell needs an even width and height. Neighbours outside the map count as empty unless `outside: "match"`. A legend entry set to `null` marks context cells: they are never drawn but count for other entries' `match`, so a review window cut from a larger map can carry a one-tile ring of the surrounding terrain (placed at a negative `at`) and its edge tiles still see their real neighbours. Every referenced name is checked when the scene is prepared; errors name the cell, e.g. `tilemap.rows[2][5]`. Masks and quarter pieces are the same ones the autotile compiler uses. `weights` (one whole number per name) make some variants commoner than others, and a tilemap `seed` reshuffles every seeded pick.

`layout: "iso"` lays the map on a 2:1 diamond grid instead, with an even `tile` such as `[32, 16]`: the character at column x of row y is cell [x, y], whose diamond has its top-left at `[(x - y + rows - 1) * 16, (x + y) * 8]` in map pixels, so the map's top-left is its placement point. Cells draw back to front. Each frame stands on its diamond, bottoms aligned and centred across, so a 32×16 floor tile fills it and a 32×32 block rises 16 pixels above it. Draw the frames with the `box` operation. Masks and rules work on the character grid as before. Something standing in cell [x, y] stands at the diamond's centre, `[(x - y + rows) * 16, (x + y + 1) * 8]` plus the map's placement point, for a 32×16 tile. `guides: {"iso": {"tile": [32, 16], "at": <cell [0, 0]'s diamond top-left>}}` draws the grid over a review. With `sort: "ground"`, an iso tile stands just above its diamond's centre: a placement standing in a cell behind a block is covered by it, one in front covers it, and one standing on top of it (in its cell, with `lift`) is drawn over it.

A legend entry can instead give `rules`, which choose by the characters around a cell rather than a same-or-different mask: what a 3/4 room needs, where a wall casts a face onto the floor below it and an island hangs a cliff into the void. Each rule draws what a plain entry draws (`frame`, `frames`, `animation` or `animations`, with `weights`, `autotile` and `match`) and may add:

- `where`: three rows of three characters centred on the cell. A dot matches anything; the tilemap's `empty` character matches empty cells and cells beyond the map (or anything there, with `outside: "match"`); a character from the tilemap's `classes` (`{"#": "WV"}`) matches any of its members, and may include the empty character; any other character matches itself.
- `chance`: a seeded probability that a matching rule draws, for wear and clutter.
- `offset`: `[columns, rows]` to draw into another cell. An offset output draws over that cell's own tiles.
- `stop`: when this rule draws, the cell's later rules are skipped.

Every rule that matches draws, in order. Tiles are drawn in map order of the cell they land in. A wall's cap and the cliff below it are one entry:

```json
"W": { "rules": [
  { "frames": ["cap-{mask}", "capb-{mask}"], "weights": [2, 1], "autotile": "blob" },
  { "where": ["...", "...", ".~."], "frame": "cliff", "offset": [0, 1] }
] }
```

Recipes draw the same legends with the `tilemap` operation (symbols and autotile templates instead of frames), so a room can be composed inside one recipe and shaded, dithered and rewritten afterwards; see the [authoring guide](agent-guide.md#operations). The [diorama study](../showcase/diorama/README.md) builds a whole room both ways.

`inspectTile(renderProject(recipe), frameName)` and `inspect --view tile` return a 3×3 repeat plus advisory seam evidence per axis. `doubledRows` (left/right wrap) and `doubledColumns` (top/bottom wrap) list lines where both opposite edges carry the same color that differs from its inner neighbours, which doubles into a 2px seam. `wrapSteps` counts large value steps across the wrap, compared with `interiorMaxSteps`, the busiest interior boundary. `suspicious` is true when a quarter or more of the lines double, or the wrap has more large steps than any interior boundary (a gradient or texture that does not continue). A stroke that merely touches one edge is not flagged. This is evidence to inspect, not proof that a tile is defective. Distinguish spatial variants from animations by selecting explicit frames in scene placements. Fractional scales are rejected; larger integer source pixels are highlighted for review. There is no automated style/palette harmonization, ground-speed solver or general scene editor.

## Depth and perspective

A recipe or a scene is flat pixels drawn in order, and a scene's camera only pans. Depth comes from choices made the same way in every asset of a set, so settle them in the brief before drawing.

**One projection.** Mixing projections is the quickest way for a set to look wrong: a floor seen from above beside a crate seen straight on.

| Projection | Ground and walls | Props and figures | Normal `faces` |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Side view | The ground is a row; walls stand upright | Seen straight on, standing on their bottom row | tops `w`, fronts `5` |
| 3/4 top-down | Floor seen from above; walls show a cap and a front face (tilemap `rules`) | Seen from a shallow front angle: a box shows its top and its front | tops `w` (or `8` seen from higher), south faces `2` (or `5` seen from lower) |
| Isometric 2:1 | Diamond tiles twice as wide as tall; edges step 2 pixels across for each pixel down (`box`, tilemap `layout: "iso"`) | A box shows its top and two sides (`box`) | tops `w`, left faces `z`, right faces `c` |
| Top-down | Everything seen from above | Shapes are plans; height shows only in shadows | tops `5` |

**Depth cues and the tool for each.**

- *Overlap.* What stands nearer draws over what stands farther. Put everything that stands on the ground in one sorted group (scene placements and tilemaps of walls or trees, or the operations of a recipe layer, with `sort: "ground"`), each placed by an anchor at its feet or base (a recipe or frame `anchor`, and `anchor: "frame"` on the placement). In a side view a figure's far limbs are a ramp step darker and stay behind the body; `swapDepth` is for front and back views.
- *Contact.* Whatever touches the ground needs a contact shadow, or it floats: a `shade` ellipse under a prop in a recipe, or a `shade` placement attached to an actor's `feet` point.
- *Height.* Something off the ground is drawn higher but stands where it would land: give a jumping or flying placement `lift`, and attach its shadow without a point so the shadow stays on the ground below it.
- *Cast shadows.* A shadow falling away from a light ties what casts it to the ground and shows where the light is: give lit props and figures `shadow: true`, with a light high and wide enough to light the floor around them, or a sun outdoors.
- *Form.* Shade from one light direction along `ramps`; scene lights have a `height` above the ground. Give boxes, walls and buildings generated normals with `faces` ([generated normal passes](#generated-normal-passes)) so their planes stay flat.
- *Value and detail with distance.* Far planes have less contrast and fewer details, with values closer to the sky's; the darkest darks and brightest lights belong to the near planes. Give far placements `haze` (scene `haze` sets the colour) rather than scaling them; it fades each palette colour as a whole, so the art keeps its palette structure. Draw a simpler distant version when a near asset has more detail than its far size can carry. Check with `view: "grayscale"`.
- *Motion.* When the view pans, far planes slide less than near ones: give a scene a `camera` and each plane a `parallax` (0.25 and 0.5 behind, 1 for the scene, 1.5 for anything between it and the viewer).
- *Size.* Farther is smaller only when it is drawn smaller: placement `scale` enlarges by whole numbers and fractional scale is refused.
- *Lines.* Receding edges meet at a vanishing point, and isometric edges run 2:1. A `line` from (0, 0) to (2n, n) steps evenly; endpoints off that ratio give uneven steps.

**Review.**

- **Depth view.** `pixel_scene` with `view: "depth"` (CLI `pixelbolt scene room.scene.json --image depth.png --view depth --time 900`; the studio's **Depth** box) tints each placement by when it is drawn, cool for far and warm for near, and leaves the ground grey: placements that lie straight on the background, such as a floor, a backdrop or a tilemap under everything. Each sorted placement shows its ground line in white across its width, with a tick at its placement point, and dashes from the tick up to a lifted placement. A line across the top of a tree means its anchor is on the wrong row, and it sorts behind everything standing in front of its trunk. The reply's `depth` lists the sorted placements far to near with the point each stands on (`at`) and any `lift`, a sorted tilemap as one item per row of tiles (`{name, ground, tiles}`, its line running under each tile), and `overlaps`: who covers whom, by how many pixels, largest first. The placement behind is never the ground, sorted placements always count, and a name several placements share gets `#index`, its place in `instances`. When nothing is sorted, a `note` says so. Render the view at the moments things cross, such as a hero walking past a barrel.
- **Guides.** `guides` on `pixel_inspect` and `pixel_scene` (CLI `--guides` on `inspect` and `scene --image`) draw over the enlarged image, never into the art: `horizon` (a row), `vanish` (one to three points drawn as rays; they may lie off the canvas) and `iso` (`{"tile": [32, 16], "at": [0, 0]}`: the diagonals of diamond tiles whose bounding box starts at `at`). Compare the drawn edges with them: `{"horizon": 40, "vanish": [[64, 40]]}` checks a one-point room, and two points on the horizon a building's corner.

**Not built in.** There is no zoom, rotation or perspective projection (the camera only pans), and haze is per placement, not a gradient across the scene. A shadow lies flat on the ground as a cutout's, never across walls or figures, and a sun keeps one direction for the whole scene. Inside one recipe, draw parallax as frames whose layers move by different `x` each frame (far layers least) and recolour a far layer with a layer `palette`; draw each shadow.

## Autotile templates

A `pixelforge-autotile` source ([autotile.schema.json](../autotile.schema.json)) turns one small drawing into a complete terrain set. `mode: "blob"` (default) builds 47 tiles from eight neighbours (N=1, NE=2, E=4, SE=8, S=16, SW=32, W=64, NW=128; a diagonal counts only when both adjacent sides match). `mode: "cardinal"` builds 16 tiles from four neighbours (N=1, E=2, S=4, W=8) without inner corners. `mode: "corner"` builds 16 tiles for a dual grid: each sits where four map cells meet, its mask says which of them are terrain (NW=1, NE=2, SW=4, SE=8), and each of its quarters is the inner quarter of one of those cells, cut from the same template. A corner map draws exactly what a blob map of the same cells draws, from a third of the tiles; variants are picked per tile, so they change in the middle of cells. Mask 0 draws nothing and is compiled once, because Godot's painter puts it on a painted cell that has no terrain point. `frame` names each tile with `{mask}`. Tiles are even, 2 to 84 pixels.

The drawing is a template two tiles wide and three tall, laid out like an RPG Maker A2 autotile:

```text
[ preview (unused) ][ inner corners  ]   each quarter of the inner-corner tile is that quarter's inner corner
[   2×2-tile island: outer corners, edges and fill   ]
```

Each tile is stitched from four quarters of it, and they are not neighbours in the template: a top-edge tile is the island's top-right quarter followed by its top-left one, and the fill tile is the island's middle with its quarters swapped. So the template has to repeat every tile: whatever leaves one quarter has to continue into the quarter that follows it in the island, and the notches of the inner-corner tile have to meet the island's edges. The island's middle lines (column `tile`, row `2 × tile`) become tile borders in a map, so detail may cross them; the quarter lines inside each tile may not.

**Describe the terrain instead of typing the template.** A `terrain` draws the template from a description that cannot seam, and is the quickest way to a good set. Start from `examples/meadow.autotile.json` (grass over dirt, seen from above) or `examples/ground.autotile.json` (platformer ground seen from the side: a grass cap, lit walls, roots underneath):

```json
{ "format": "pixelforge-autotile", "version": 1, "name": "meadow", "tile": 16, "frame": "meadow-{mask}",
  "palette": { "k": "#1e2b22", "D": "#2d4a2c", "d": "#3c6b35", "g": "#4f8a3f", "G": "#67a447", "s": "#20140c59", "u": "#7a5534" },
  "terrain": { "seed": 4,
    "fill": { "color": "g", "speckle": [{ "color": "d", "amount": 0.22, "size": 5 }, { "color": "G", "amount": 0.16, "size": 4 }] },
    "edges": { "all": { "depth": 2, "jitter": 1, "bands": ["k", "d"] },
               "n": { "bands": ["k", "G"], "tufts": { "colors": ["g", "G"], "amount": 0.4 } },
               "s": { "depth": 3, "bands": ["k", "D"], "outside": ["s"] } },
    "round": 2, "inner": 1 },
  "preview": { "background": "u" } }
```

- `fill` is a palette key, `{ "color", "speckle" }` or `{ "pattern" }`, and repeats every tile. Each speckle layer colours exactly its `amount` of the tile in seeded clumps about `size` pixels across, in order; `pattern` is tile × tile rows. `fill.seed` (default the terrain's `seed`) reseeds only the speckle.
- `edges` describes each side: `n` top, `e` right, `s` bottom, `w` left, with `all` for every side and a side's own fields over it. `depth` is the margin between the tile border and the terrain, and `jitter` lets the edge wander that many pixels further in (seeded, with a turn every `size` pixels). `bands` colour the terrain from the edge inward (an outline, then a rim light or a shade); `outside` colours the margin from the edge outward (a cast shadow, in a key with alpha); `tufts` grow seeded blades out of the edge into its margin, 1 to `colors.length` pixels long on `amount` of the columns (grass on a top edge, roots or drips under a bottom one). Decorations stay in their own side's margin and stop a pixel short of the tile border, so give a side with tufts enough `depth`.
- Edges and fill repeat every tile, so keep `jitter` at 1 on long edges (2 shows a tile-wide rhythm) and use variants to vary the fill. Speckle clumps of `size` 2 read as grain; 3 can read as a repeated little cross, and 5 or more as soft patches.
- `round` and `inner` round the outer and inner corners. Each edge's `depth + jitter + bands + round` has to fit in half a tile, the part of the tile its quarters know about; the error says by how much it does not.
- `ops` draw over the template (rows or terrain) on its own canvas, two tiles wide and three tall, with the source's `symbols` and `ramps`: flowers, cracks, stones. Every colour drawn needs a one-character palette key. Keep each detail inside one quarter, or across the island's middle lines. A quarter of the island's middle (columns `tile / 2` to `3 × tile / 2`, rows `3 × tile / 2` to `5 × tile / 2`) is fill in every tile that uses it, edge tiles included, so a detail there turns up often: put it in a variant and weight that variant low in the map legend.
- A variant's `terrain` merges into the set's field by field: `{ "fill": { "seed": 11 } }` redraws the fill and keeps every edge pixel, so variants mix in a map without seams. A variant's `ops` draw after the set's. A variant's `weight` (1–1000, default 1) says how often it is picked against the others: give the plain fills 4 and a flowered one 1. The test map mixes variants by weight, and the Godot TileSet gives each tile that probability.
- `collision` gives every tile a collision box for the Godot TileSet: `true` boxes the whole tile, kept from each open side by that edge's `depth` (so a body stands on the drawn grass top, not on the tufts above it), and `{ "inset": 2 }` or `{ "inset": { "n": 3 } }` sets the margins, under half a tile. `"oneWay": true` makes one-way platforms that bodies jump up through and land on. A corner tile is solid in the quarters of its terrain cells, each kept from that cell's open sides, so its shapes join into the blob boxes of the same map; where two terrain cells touch only at the tile's middle it gets two polygons. Each compiled frame carries its box as `tile.collision` (below). A variant may instead bring its own `template` rows (cracked or mossy stone with the base set's edges), or only a frame `palette` and `duration` for palette-cycled animation (surf, glowing lava), with `{variant}` in the frame name and an optional per-mask `animation`. Variants with templates of their own compile to the symbols `template-<name>`, and a tilemap legend mixes them with `"frames": ["wall-{mask}-plain", "wall-{mask}-cracked"], "weights": [2, 1]`.

**Review the set over its test map.** Compiling also draws every tile over a test map of 20 × 14 cells that holds all 47 blob tiles, and so every cardinal and corner one (a block with holes in every arrangement of inner corners, strips and their ends, a lone tile, tiles that touch only at a corner, L and T shapes), on `preview.background` when given: pass `--preview <new.png>` to the CLI, while MCP `pixel_compile` returns it as a second image. Look at it for repetition, stray pixels and edges that read wrong. The compiler also checks every join a map can make: a `Seam` warning names template pixels where an outline or edge breaks and the pixel to copy to mend it, and one `Texture` warning counts fill that does not repeat (fine speckle hides that; lines and clumps break visibly). What a terrain draws is seamless by construction, so on a terrain only what `ops` change is checked, and, where a variant's tiles meet the set's, any edge its terrain draws in another place (another `depth`, `jitter`, `round` or `seed`): another fill or outline colour switches at the tile border, as a palette variant does. A cardinal set has no inner-corner tiles, so where two edges meet at an inner corner they stop at the tile border; it says so once. Use blob for outlined terrain, or cardinal with edges that run to the border (depth 0, no bands).

```sh
node bin/pixelbolt.js autotile examples/meadow.autotile.json --out meadow.json --preview meadow-map.png
```
- `slopes: { "frame": "ground-{slope}" }` gives a side-view blob or cardinal set slopes for a platformer, cut from its own top edge and fill: `up` and `down` rise and fall 45° (a cell up over a cell across, read left to right), and `gentle-up-low`, `gentle-up-high`, `gentle-down-high` and `gentle-down-low` are the halves of a slope a cell up over two. Each column of a slope is the flat ground's top-edge column (margin, tufts, bands) moved down the slope, over the fill tile drawn in place, so slopes meet the flat ground and its fill texture without a seam. A flat tile's ground starts its north edge's `depth` below the cell, so a slope's low end runs into the cell under it: that cell takes the slope's `<slope>-foot` tile (`up-foot`, `down-foot` and the gentle low halves' feet). A slope stands on terrain that goes on below it and toward its high side. With `collision`, a slope is solid under its line, the edge's `depth` (its `inset`) below it, so a body walks on the drawn ground; `oneWay` carries over. The compiled frames stamp the symbols `slope-<slope>`, and `metadata.slopes` holds a scene `legend` and a `map`: `#` is terrain (each cell under a slope's low end draws its foot, and masks count slopes as terrain), `/` and `\` the 45° slopes, `u U` a gentle rise (low, high) and `D d` a gentle fall (high, low), placed as in `["...../##\\..", "..../####\\.", "###########"]` (a 45° slope one row down and one across from the next). The test map adds those hills below the set.

Every compiled frame is one `autotile` operation naming its neighbour mask, drawn from the `template` symbol, so a frame reads as `{"op": "autotile", "symbol": "template", "mask": 23}` and editing the template in the compiled recipe updates the whole set. Use the same masks in a scene `tilemap` legend (`"autotile": "blob"`, or the set's mode) or in a game. Exporting the compiled set also writes `name.tileset.tres`, a Godot 4 TileSet whose terrain paints with these masks ([tile sets](game-integration.md#tile-sets)). A frame's `tile` holds what the TileSet needs beyond pixels: `weight`, a `collision` polygon (or a list of them) in canvas pixels and `oneWay`, and `terrain`, which keeps two sets in one recipe apart.

**Transitions between terrains.** `layers` stacks 2–4 terrains bottom first (water, sand, grass) into one corner set with a tile for every mix of them at a tile's four corners: 16 tiles for two, 81 for three, 256 for four (as many frames as a recipe holds, so four layers take no `variants`). Each layer has a `name` (the terrain's name in engines), a one-character `key`, a letter or digit unique among the layers ignoring letter case (its character in maps and tile names) and its own `terrain` (merged over the set's `terrain`, when it has one), `template` rows or `ops`. `frame` names each tile with `{corners}`, the layers' keys at its NW, NE, SW and SE corners, so `shore-wsgw` has water at NW and SE, sand at NE and grass at SW. A tile is the lowest terrain among its corners filling it, and each higher one among them drawn over it as a corner tile counting its own cells and every higher terrain's, so sand runs on under grass and a transition only shows between terrains that meet: grass next to water draws no sand. Each frame's `tile.corners` names its corners' terrains. Start from `examples/shore.autotile.json`. Draw a layered map in a scene with one legend entry per key, all `{ "frame": "shore-{corners}", "autotile": "corner" }` (every cell a key; past the map's edge the nearest cell counts), or in a recipe with one `tilemap` per layer, bottom first, whose legend draws that layer's template from its own key with `match` set to its key and every higher one, `outside: "match"`, and `null` for the other keys; both draw the same pixels.

A layer's `variants` vary its plain tile, the one with it at all four corners, which fills big areas. Each has a `name`, a `weight` against the layer's own `weight` for the plain tile (both default 1), and a `terrain` merged into the layer's (`{ "fill": { "seed": 11 } }` is another fill) or `ops` drawn over the plain tile in tile pixels (a lily pad, a shell), or both. Keep ops a pixel inside the tile: the tiles beside it don't continue them, and the compiler warns. Variant tiles come after the others, named after the plain tile with `-<name>` (`shore-wwww-lily`); transitions keep the layer's own fill. In a scene, list them for the key with their weights, `{ "frames": ["shore-{corners}", "shore-{corners}-b", "shore-{corners}-lily"], "weights": [4, 4, 1], "autotile": "corner" }`: at each point the names the set has no tile for are left out, so a variant only lands where four cells of its terrain meet. A layer's `collision` (`true`, or `{ inset, oneWay }`) makes it solid where it shows: its corners, from where its edges start, less those of the terrains drawn over it, from where theirs start, so solid water stops where the sand begins. A layered set doesn't animate. Its test map puts the top terrain's shapes over the bottom one, rings of the terrains between round its big block and a patch of each of them below, with the variants where a scene with those legends puts them.

## Particle effects

A `pixelforge-fx` source ([fx.schema.json](../fx.schema.json)) describes seeded particle emitters, after Pixel Composer's particle system, and compiles them into an ordinary recipe: every frame becomes `stamp`, `grid` and `line` operations on the source's own symbols. Inspect, patch, overlay and export the result like any other recipe; editing a symbol in it redraws every particle that uses it.

```json
{ "format": "pixelforge-fx", "version": 1, "name": "fx", "width": 48, "height": 40,
  "palette": { "W": "#fff4d6", "y": "#ffd166", "o": "#f4843c", "r": "#c2413a" },
  "symbols": { "hot": ["W"], "warm": ["y"], "cool": ["o"], "ash": ["r"] },
  "effects": { "sparks": { "frames": 9, "duration": 55, "seed": 11, "emitters": [
    { "at": [24, 30], "burst": 12, "angle": [205, 335], "speed": [1.8, 3.4], "gravity": [0, 0.32], "life": [6, 9],
      "floor": 36, "bounce": 0.35, "shapes": ["hot", "warm", "cool", "ash"], "trail": { "color": "r", "length": 1 } } ] } } }
```

```sh
node bin/pixelbolt.js compile examples/effects.fx.json --out output/effects.json --metadata output/effects.meta.json
node bin/pixelbolt.js preview examples/effects.fx.json   # the studio shows the compiled recipe
```

The studio's **Effects lab** (`/effects.html`) compiles the reviewed spell and particle presets in the browser: pick one of a source's effects, change its strength (emitter rates and bursts), speed (frame timing) and seed, and download the GIF, sprite sheet, recipe or edited source, with the matching CLI and MCP calls beside them.

Each effect becomes frames `<effect>-0`, `<effect>-1`… and an animation named after the effect. Each emitter says:

- **When it spawns.** A `burst` at frame `start`, or a `rate` of particles per frame until `end`; 2–8 rate keys, such as `[4, 0]`, ramp it from `start` to `end` (a spray that starts thick and tapers).
- **Where.** Inside an `area` box centred on `at`. In place of `at`, a `path` of 2–16 points moves the spawn centre through them over the emitter's window from `start` to `end`, timed as a layer's keys are over its frames, through the emitter's `ease` (default linear) as a layer's go through its own, so a trail with the same window and `ease` as a layer's `path` keeps pace with it: a meteor sheds smoke as it falls, a spark traces a circle (round a whole loop, end the path where it starts). The particles a frame spawns spread along the stretch travelled since the last frame, so a fast trail stays even; children spawning while a parent lives spread the same way along the parent's last step. `inherit` on a moving emitter hands its particles that share of its own velocity along the path, so sparks thrown by a projectile carry forward. A path is travelled at a steady speed along its length, so points far apart take longer to pass between than points close together and the spawn centre never lurches. `curve: true` runs a path of three or more points through a smooth curve that still passes every point, so an arc has no corner at its middle; on a layer it does the same for its `path` or keyed `drift` (drift keys, unlike a path, each take an equal share of the window).
- **How particles move.** Per-particle values are picked from `[min, max]` ranges: `angle` (degrees clockwise, 0 right, 90 down, 270 up), `speed` (pixels per frame) and `life` (frames). `sweep`, 2–8 keys in degrees over the emitter's window, turns the launch angle as it goes: a flamethrower swings across (`[-40, 40]`), a fountain sways. `throttle` keys scale the launch speed the same way, so a fountain weakens (`[1, 0.3]`) or a flamethrower dies out. Each frame velocity gains `gravity` and an optional `attract` pull, loses `drag`, and moves the particle; a `floor` stops it, with optional `bounce`; `ground` is a floor that many pixels below where each particle was born instead, so shards thrown up from a squashed ring land back on their own patch of the ellipse. `attract.swirl` adds a push across the line to the point, clockwise when positive, so particles spiral into a portal or circle a healing target (a mirrored turn swirls the other way). `turbulence: { strength, scale }` pushes each particle by a smooth seeded field that stays put, `scale` pixels to a swirl (default 8) and at most `strength` pixels per frame per frame (default 0.3): smoke and embers wander apart organically instead of swaying in step, and a still field keeps loops seamless. Give them a little `drag` (0.05–0.1) too, or a long life keeps gathering speed. `sway` adds a drawn wobble (x follows a sine, y a cosine) that never carries a particle below its floor.
- **How they look.** `shapes` is one symbol sequence, or several with one picked per particle; `play` spreads it over the life (`life`, default), steps one per frame from a random start (`loop`, for flutter) or steps and holds (`once`, for hand-drawn flashes). `spin` (degrees a frame, a number or `[min, max]`) turns each particle's symbol from a seeded start in 45° RotSprite steps, so shards, rocks and leaves tumble; the turned symbols join the recipe as `<symbol>_r<degrees>`, so a spinning symbol's visible width plus height may be at most 360 pixels (its 45° turns must fit a recipe symbol). Spin chunky symbols, 4–7 pixels across with a dark outline: a thin sliver turns into a one-pixel diagonal line at 45° and reads as a spark. `remaps` gives colour variants, `trail` draws a one-pixel streak from an earlier position, and `dissolve` thins the last share of each life through an ordered-dither `pattern` (a Bayer name, `noise` ordered by the effect's `seed`, or a custom rank matrix, as for the `dither` operation; `[[0, 1, 2], [1, 2, 0], [2, 0, 1]]` dissolves along diagonals).

**Shapes of a burst.** Instead of `area`, an emitter can spawn on a `ring` around `at` (`radius`, a number or `[min, max]`; `arc`, `[from, to]` degrees, default the whole circle) or along a `line` from `at` to `to`. `even: true` spaces the particles spawned together evenly (a full ring has no repeat at its end; an arc or line reaches both ends) instead of at random; on a rate emitter a full even ring turns to a fresh seeded angle each frame, so its spokes don't repeat. `kick: [x, y]` adds one velocity to every launch, whatever its angle: with `[0, -1.5]`, outward shards on a ring laid on the ground pop up as they fly out (and `ground` lands them back on it). On a ring with `outward: true`, `angle` is measured from each particle's outward direction: 0 (the default) launches straight out, 90 along the ring clockwise and 180 inward. A ring's `squash` (`[1, 0.4]`, say) lays it on the ground as a ring layer's does, so sparks rise from a magic circle's ellipse, spread evenly along its perimeter rather than crowding its narrow ends; outward then points away from its middle along the ellipse. With `fill: true` the distance is picked evenly by area, so `radius: [0, 20]` fills a disc evenly instead of crowding its middle. A ring's `grow`, 2–8 keys over the emitter's window timed as a layer's are, scales its radius as it goes: with the same window, `ease` and keys as a ring layer's radius (as shares of the largest), sparks ride the shockwave's edge out. To match a layer with `frames: [1, 11]`, give the emitter `start: 1, end: 11` (the same numbers: both name the frame after the last) and `ease: "out"`, since a one-shot layer's ease defaults to `out` and an emitter's to `linear`. To stop spawning early, keep that window and end keyed `rate` at 0 (`[1.5, 1.5, 0.5, 0, 0]`): a shorter window would squeeze the whole `grow` into it, and the particles would outrun the ring. A burst spawns at one moment, so `path`, `grow`, `sweep` and `throttle` need a `rate` emitter. An even, outward ring of a dozen particles with strong `drag` is a shockwave; an outward arc with a little angle jitter is a fan of sparks.

**Particles that point where they go.** With `face: true`, `shapes` lists 2–16 sequences by direction of travel instead of variants picked at random: the first faces right and the rest turn clockwise, evenly spaced (with eight: right, down-right, down, down-left, left, up-left, up, up-right). Each frame a particle draws the sequence closest to its velocity, so a spark is a streak along its arc as gravity bends it, and one at rest keeps its last direction. For a shockwave, draw the segment across the direction (a vertical dash for a particle moving right) and the ring reads as a circle. `tint` lists remaps spread over each life like `play: "life"`, the first at birth: a white-hot streak cools through yellow and orange to red without a symbol per colour, and each stage overrides the particle's own `remap` only for the characters it names.

**Particles that make particles.** A child emitter names an earlier emitter with `from` (names must then be unique) and spawns from its particles instead of on its own schedule: `when: "death"` (the default) bursts where a parent particle ends, `"floor"` where it first lands on its floor (it bounces on without spawning again), and `"life"` emits `rate` particles a frame from each parent while it lives, a trail. A child's `at` is an offset from its parent (default none), `area`, `ring` and `line` work around that point, and `inherit` adds a share of the parent's velocity. Children have no `start` or `end`. Their random draws are keyed by their parent, so a looping effect stays seamless; it warms up for the longest chain of lives. `behind: true` draws an emitter's particles under everyone else's: smoke behind sparks, a trail behind its bolt.

**Timing and cues.** `duration` may be `{ "from": 40, "to": 110, "ease": "in" }`: frame durations eased across the effect. `in` keeps the first frames quick and slows toward the end, the usual shape of an impact (a fast flash and burst, a lingering settle). `cue` names a point at an emitter's spawn centre in every frame it spawns, such as `impact` on the flash or `land` on debris hitting the floor. Frames carry cues as named points, the atlas exports them, and the metadata lists them with their frames, so game code can shake the camera or pause for a few frames exactly on the hit.

**Spell and impact shapes.** An effect may also carry `layers`: the stylized shapes that particles draw badly, each in one line. A `ring` is a shockwave (a crisp leading edge at `radius` that fades inward over `width`; `fill` glows inside it and `noise` breaks it up), a `slash` a crescent (a crisp outer arc at `radius`, thickest mid-`arc` and pointed at both ends, whose head sweeps the arc over the first `reveal` share of its frames while the tail chases it), a `beam` a straight beam from `at` to `to` (`reveal` shoots it out, `wobble`, `wavelength` and `waves` ripple it, `fade` dims it toward the far end for a pillar of light, and `squash` flattens its round ends alone), a `bolt` lightning (a jagged one-pixel core of `width` with a `glow`, `branches` spread down it on alternate sides, a new path every `reroll` frames, and `reveal` to strike down), a `glyph` a magic circle (an outer ring, `double` for a second one two pixels inside, an `inner` ring, upright 3×3 seeded `runes` between them that travel round with `spin`, and a star polygon of `points` corners joined every `step`, 0 for none; it draws on clockwise from the top), a `star` an impact star (`points` spikes, every other one `ratio` as long, tapering to one-pixel tips around a `core`), a `burst` a fireball (a disc broken by seeded smooth `noise` that boils outward, hottest in the middle, with a `hole` that burns out from the centre), an `aura` a glow licking up round a sprite, and a `trail` the smear behind a moving point such as a sword tip (both under effects on a sprite, below).

```json
"slash-hit": { "frames": 9, "duration": { "from": 40, "to": 90, "ease": "in" }, "seed": 3,
  "looks": { "steel": { "colors": ["b", "c", "p", "W"], "thresholds": [0.15, 0.4, 0.65, 0.88] },
             "flash": { "colors": ["o", "y", "W"], "thresholds": [0.2, 0.5, 0.8] } },
  "cleanup": { "corners": true, "strays": true },
  "layers": [
    { "kind": "slash", "look": "steel", "at": [38, 42], "frames": [0, 8], "radius": 32, "width": [15, 11],
      "arc": [195, 345], "squash": [1, 0.68], "tilt": -16, "reveal": 0.3 },
    { "kind": "star", "look": "flash", "at": [64, 27], "frames": [1, 6], "radius": [18, 5], "width": [3, 1],
      "power": [1.3, 0.6], "cue": "hit" },
    { "kind": "ring", "look": "flash", "at": [64, 27], "frames": [2, 7], "radius": [4, 13], "width": [4, 1], "power": [1.1, 0.4] } ],
  "emitters": [ { "at": [64, 27], "burst": 12, "start": 2, "angle": [150, 390], "speed": [1.5, 3.2], "gravity": [0, 0.25],
    "drag": 0.05, "life": [4, 7], "shapes": ["spark", "spark-y", "spark-o"], "trail": { "color": "o", "length": 1 } } ] }
```

Each layer writes a brightness field during its window, `frames: [first, end]` (the frame after the last; default the whole effect), over which its progress runs from 0 toward 1. A numeric field written as 2–8 keys (`"radius": [4, 30]`, `"width": [0, 12, 10, 0]`) moves through them across the window with the layer's `ease` (default `out`); reveals count to the end of each frame, so a window's first frame already shows the shape under way. `looks` name colour ramps: the layers that name a look combine (the brighter wins, or `blend: "add"` sums them) and its `thresholds` cut the result into hard bands of its `colors`, faintest first, with an optional ordered `dither` feathering each band in below its threshold. Looks draw in order, later over earlier; then `cleanup` removes doubled corners and stray pixels from the shapes and each look's `outline` takes the empty pixels beside its own. `power` multiplies the field, so as it falls the bright bands shrink and the shape erodes instead of fading, and `flicker` dims every other frame. `squash: [1, 0.4]` lays a ring or a magic circle on the ground (2–8 pairs are keys, so a pool can round out as it shrinks instead of thinning to a sliver), `tilt` turns a slash onto a diagonal (neither applies to a bolt, a stream or a trail, which run between their points, or to an aura, which hugs its sprite), `drift` moves a layer over its window (2–8 offsets are keys, so an orb can arc or a flame wander instead of sliding straight; in place of `at` and `drift`, a `path` of absolute points does the same, so a projectile's head can share its emitters' path), and `cue` marks the layer's first frame as an emitter's cue does. The shapes become one `grid` per frame between `behind` particles and the rest, so sparks, embers and cues draw over them; an effect may have layers and no emitters, and a source with no emitters needs no `symbols` (but for an aura's sprite). Noise, runes and bolt paths are seeded by the effect's `seed` and each layer's own `seed` (default its index), and the maths is the same polynomial arithmetic as the particles', so a source always compiles to the same pixels.

Give a hit one strong shape for a frame or two (a star, the slash's full crescent, the bolt's first strike), then let it erode by lowering `power` or `width` rather than dithering it away. Keep a look to three to five colours with the brightest on the leading edge. End a ring's `width` at 2 or more and give it some `noise`, so a fading shockwave breaks up instead of thinning to a plain outline. Keep a squashed `glyph` simple: `double` rings, eight to twelve runes and a small polygon (`points: 4, step: 1`) read on the ground where a hexagram turns into a tangle. Stand a pillar of light on the circle's centre: the beam's `at` on the glyph's `at`, with the glyph's `squash`, so its foot lies on the same ground instead of reaching toward the front ring. List the floor's look before the pillar's (a floor never covers what stands on it), and give sparkles that rise from the far half of the circle an emitter of their own with `behind: true`, so they pass behind the pillar while the near half's pass in front. For smoke after a fireball, use blob particles (described under water below) in a grey look with `lit`, `shade` and a dark `outline`, rising slowly with some `drag` and radius keys that swell and then shrink. They merge into one lit cloud and fade by breaking into shrinking puffs. List that look after the fire's, so the smoke swallows the dying fire from above, and start it once the flash is over. That outlined smoke suits a cel style with hard outlines everywhere. Gas, mist and thin smoke read as vapour only when they are see-through. Give their look colours with alpha (`#rrggbbaa`, about 35 % opaque at the edge to 65 % at the core), a `lit` band on top, and no `shade` band, `outline` or `dither`: a shadow side and a dark rim make a cloud read as a rock, and a dithered edge as speckle. Let `turbulence` drift the puffs apart, and blob `power` keys (`[1, 1, 0.8, 0.5]`) thin them as they swell. The gas in `examples/poison-spells.fx.json` works this way. PNG sheets keep the translucency; a GIF can't, so give it a `--background`. A look's `halo: { color, width, dither }` glows on the empty pixels within a round reach of it, beyond its outline, dithered on every other pixel from `width: 2` (a one-pixel glow is solid, since a checkerboard on a one-pixel ring leaves whole diagonal sides solid or empty; `dither` overrides either way), so a bright orb glows without a hard ring. Looks draw under the symbol particles; give a look `front: true` to draw it over them instead, as smoke that rolls over the sparks (cleanup and outlines then work within each pass). Smoke and fire that should move like a fluid look better from `pixelforge-fluid`. Eight reviewed effects to start from are in `examples/`: `slash-hit.fx.json`, `fire-explosion.fx.json`, `fireball.fx.json` (a seamless loop in eight directions), `lightning-strike.fx.json`, `healing-circle.fx.json` and `vortex.fx.json` (a seamless loop whose motes swirl in with `attract.swirl` and whose wisps wander with `turbulence`, cool with blob `power` and draw in front of the motes), and `meteor.fx.json` (a one-shot whose head shares its `path` and window with the trail emitters, so smoke, a tail of cooling fire blobs and embers keep pace with it before an impact that throws tumbling rock chips with `spin`), and `shield.fx.json` (a looping bubble whose ring churns with `boil` inside a dithered `halo`).

**Looping shapes.** In a looping effect (`loop: true`), a layer whose window spans every frame (the default) is a cycle: the frame after its last is its first. It moves at a steady pace (`ease` defaults to `linear`) and is whole from frame 0 (`reveal` defaults to 0). A `burst`'s noise boils outward by `boil` pixels every cycle without slowing, through two noise fields half a cycle apart that take turns, so a fireball in flight, a burning patch or an idle aura has no jump at the loop point. The rest has to meet up too, and that is up to the source:
- keys that end where they start (`"power": [1, 0.8, 1]`);
- a `spin` that brings the shape back onto itself, such as a whole turn or a whole number of rune steps (a glyph's star turns the other way, so it must come round as well);
- whole `waves` on a beam or stream;
- a `reroll` that divides the frame count, and an even frame count for `flicker`;
- no `drift`.

A shorter window in a loop is an event that repeats every cycle and is drawn as in a one-shot. `examples/fireball.fx.json` is a projectile in flight: a cycling burst for the ball, a beam with `fade` and two whole `waves` for its tail, and looping embers, in eight directions.

**Directions.** `turn: { "angles": [0, 45, 90, 135, 180, 225, 270, 315], "about": [28, 28] }` compiles a copy of the effect for each angle, named `<effect>-<angle>` (frames `<effect>-<angle>-0`, …). Each copy has its geometry turned about `about` (default the canvas centre) instead of its pixels rotated, so the diagonals stay as crisp as the drawn direction. The copy at 0 is the effect as drawn, pixel for pixel. A layer's noise turns with its copy, so every direction breaks up as the drawn one does, and a quarter turn is the drawn copy turned, pixel for pixel.
- What turns: places, spawn offsets, launch angles, attract points, the tilt of rings, slashes, glyphs, stars and bursts, slash arcs, beam and bolt ends, stream and trail points, and drift.
- What stays: gravity, drag, floors, sway and each look's light. A spark thrown left still falls, and a lit rim stays on the side the light comes from. A squashed ground ring would tilt with its copy, so leave ground effects unturned.
- `mirror: true` adds `<effect>-<angle>-mirror`, flipped left to right before it turns: a side-view game's left-facing hit, or a slash that sweeps the other way.
- Particle symbols are drawn as they are (give directional ones `face`), and sprite emitters and auras can't turn.

Draw the effect pointing right (angle 0) on a square canvas with room in every direction, and set the source's `anchor` to `about` so an engine places every copy by the same point. The fireball does both.

**Water and other liquids.** The same layers draw cel-shaded water, ice or slime once a look has light and shade and the shapes break up instead of fading.

- **Light and shade.** A look's `lit` and `shade` recolour its own pixels by the share by which its field falls two pixels toward the `light` (default `[-1, -1]`, the upper left) or away from it: 1 on a rim that faces that way, 0 inside. Give `lit` a near-white, `shade` the darkest water colour and `outline` something darker still, and a body gets a highlight on one rim and a shadow side on the other. A look's `reach` (default 2) is how many pixels toward the light that share is measured over: 1 keeps a small drop's highlight to a thin rim instead of covering it, and 3 or 4 spreads light across a large body.
- **Streams.** A `stream` is a tapered body along a smooth curve from `at` through up to seven more `through` points, brightest on its centre line: `head` and `tail`, shares of the curve written as keys over the window, say which part is drawn, so a geyser spike shoots up while its tail stays at 0 and a whip's tail chases its head. `taper` narrows it from tail to head (default `[0, 1, 0]`, pointed at both ends) and `sway` swings it sideways in travelling waves that grow toward the far end. `fade` dims it toward the far end of its curve, as a beam's does, so a flame or water tail cools along its length instead of ending in a bright one-pixel tip.
- **Blobs.** An emitter with `blob: { "look": …, "radius": … }` draws each particle as a soft disc added to that look's field instead of a symbol, so drops that touch merge into one body and pull apart again; `radius` keys spread over each particle's life shrink them as they fall, and `power` keys scale each disc's field over its life, so a hot drop cools through its look's bands (white, yellow, red) as it fades. A blob's field falls steeply at its edge, so a look drawn only by blobs needs `dither` around 0.6–0.8 before its feathering shows; 0.3 is barely visible. A blob emitter's `size` (a number or `[min, max]`, picked per particle) scales its radius keys, so puffs of mixed sizes billow instead of sitting as one smooth lump.
- **Noise that breaks and moves.** Every kind takes `noise` and `scale`: a pixel keeps value × power × (1 − noise × n) for seeded smooth noise n, so as `power` falls toward the look's first threshold the shape breaks into pieces and drops. A ring's leading edge has the value 1, so with `noise` 0.55 it never falls below 45 % of `power`: end `power` just above the first threshold with `noise` near 1, or the edge stays a one-pixel line. `noise` takes keys like any numeric field, so one layer can stand solid and then shatter (`"noise": [0, 0.9]`). A ring's `boil` churns its breaks over the window, or every cycle for a ring spanning a loop, seamlessly, so a shield bubble or a portal rim never stands still. On the kinds whose noise sits in screen space (slash, beam, bolt, glyph, star, stream, trail), `flow: [x, y]` moves it that many pixels over the window, or every cycle for a layer spanning a loop, seamlessly: water runs along a stream, a beam's texture streams out.
- **Floors.** A layer's `floor` cuts it off below that row: a ring with a `fill` becomes a bubble dome on the ground, and a ring an arch.

```json
"geyser": { "frames": 16, "duration": 83, "seed": 6,
  "looks": { "water": { "colors": ["m", "b"], "thresholds": [0.2, 0.55], "lit": { "colors": ["h"], "thresholds": [0.45] },
             "shade": { "colors": ["d"], "thresholds": [0.4] }, "outline": "o" } },
  "cleanup": { "corners": true, "strays": true },
  "layers": [
```

Most water effects grow to a peak shape, break into drops, and leave ripples where the drops land: lower `power` with `noise` to break the body up, start blob drops from where it breaks, and lay a squashed `ring` on the ground. Two body colours (a mid band at the edge and a pale core), one highlight, one shadow and a dark outline read as cel water at 48 to 96 pixels; more bands turn it into glass. `examples/water.fx.json` has five to start from: a geyser that falls back into its ripples, a whip, a ring that breaks into drops, a dome that pops and a curling wave.

**Effects on a sprite.** A symbol may be a frame of another recipe or pose source: `"hero": { "recipe": "hero.poses.json", "frame": "idle-rest" }` (the first frame by default). The CLI and MCP read the file, relative to the source (MCP: under the server root, or `{"revision": id}`). A pose source compiles first, and the frame becomes rows over its whole canvas, so the sprite keeps the source's coordinates. Its colours join the effect's palette under their own keys, or under spare letters where the effect already gives a key another colour. `compileEffects` takes the recipe inline.

The source may also be a fluid or wave source, compiled first: its effects are its animations. So `"blast": { "recipe": "explosion.fluid.json", "animation": "explosion" }`, drawn by a sprite emitter (below), plays a simulated fireball and its smoke inside a particle effect, frame for frame and pixel for pixel. Shape layers and particles go over it: a flash star on the first frame, a shockwave that breaks up as it spreads, embers. Give the emitter `behind: true` to draw the fluid under the shape layers.

`"animation": "walk"` in place of `frame` takes every frame of that animation, in playing order. A sprite emitter or an aura on it shows the animation's frame *i* in the effect's frame *i*: it holds the last frame after the animation ends, or goes round again in a looping effect. Give the effect the animation's frame durations so the two stay in step. An animation is a sprite, never a particle shape. It stays out of the compiled recipe's symbols, and a release takes one frame apart, not an animation.

An emitter with `sprite: { symbol }` spawns on that symbol's pixels instead of around `at`, with `at` as the sprite's top-left corner. `pixels` picks which: `all` (the default), `edge` (the visible pixels beside an empty one) or `outline` (the empty pixels just outside, as a look's outline takes them). Without `shapes` or a `blob`, each particle is its own pixel in its own colour, and `tint` stages may be one colour each (`"tint": [{}, "W", "c"]`), so a whole sprite's pixels turn to light as they fly. Pixel particles and the sprite itself draw as one grid a frame, first in their pass. With `burst` or `rate`, `draw: true` also draws the whole sprite each frame. Leave it false for an overlay that the game draws over its own sprite: sparkles from the outline, or embers rising off a burning figure. A sprite emitter with `draw: true` and no `burst`, `rate` or `release` only draws its sprite: the character under a trail or an aura, frame for frame.

`release` replaces `burst` and `rate`: every pixel leaves once between `start` and `end`, and what is left of the sprite stays drawn.
- `sweep` is the direction the release travels, in degrees clockwise; 270 takes the feet first and the head last.
- `jitter` loosens it (default 0.3; without a sweep the order is seeded at random).
- `share` turns only part of the leaving pixels into particles, while the rest just vanish on their turn. It keeps a large sprite under the particle limits.

That gives death dissolves, teleports and a sprite blown apart (`attract` with a negative strength at its middle). A release can't loop; reverse the animation (`direction: "reverse"` in the recipe) to put a sprite together.

An `aura` layer is the glow around a sprite, which particles draw badly: random dots along an outline leave gaps that flicker. It takes `symbol` and `at` (the sprite's top-left corner, as for a sprite emitter):
- It is brightest on the sprite and falls to nothing about `width` pixels out, `rise` times as far upward.
- Seeded noise, stretched upright, rises `boil` pixels over the window and moves the edge in and out, so it licks up in flames. Spanning a looping effect, it rises steadily through the loop point.
- Cut it with a look of three or four colours, faintest outside, and give the effect `cleanup`. A `floor` at the feet keeps the glow off the ground.
- Draw the sprite over it: a sprite emitter with `draw: true` in the front pass, its particles sparkles from the outline.
- In place of a symbol, `around: [rx, ry]` hugs an ellipse centred on `at`: its one-pixel ring, or with `disc: true` the whole disc. Squashed flat, it is a ring of flame or frost licking up from the ground, with no sprite to draw.

A `trail` layer is the smear behind a moving point, such as a sword tip:
- `follow: { "recipe": "swing.poses.json", "animation": "swing", "point": "blade.tip" }` takes the point's place in each frame of the animation, frame for frame. `point` is a frame's named point (a pose marker) or `part.point` for a point of a pose part, from the poses' metadata, so the in-between poses count too. `part.anchor` is where the part is fixed.
- `points` gives the places directly, one per frame of the effect (null where there is none). Both are relative to `at`, the followed sprite's top-left corner.
- In each frame the smear runs along a smooth curve through the point's last `length` places (default 3), even those before the layer's window. In a looping effect, a trail spanning the cycle reads its places round it, so frame 0 smears back through the last frames and the loop has no seam. It is `width` pixels at its newest end, tapered by `taper` (default `[0, 1]`) and dimming by `fade` toward its oldest.
- A `pivot` (`follow.pivot: "blade.anchor"`, or `[x, y]` relative to `at`, or one per frame) bends it round the point a blade sweeps round. Each step between frames is then an arc, and the smear lies inside the tip's path: a crescent with its outer edge where the tip went, instead of a curve that cuts across the swing.
- After the animation ends, the smear shrinks away over `length` frames. Cut it with a steel look of three or four colours and give the effect `cleanup`.

`examples/hero-magic.fx.json` takes the rigged hero's idle frame from `hero.poses.json`. It has a teleport (a release swept upward, tinted to light, in a pillar `beam`) and a looping spirit aura with sparkles. `examples/swing-trail.fx.json` draws the sword of `swing.poses.json` with a crescent smear round its hilt, and a flash and sparks on the strike. `examples/fluid-blast.fx.json` plays `explosion.fluid.json` under a flash star, a breaking shockwave and embers.

**Effects round a character.** A buff, an aura or a ring on the ground wraps a character the game draws itself: the far half of the ring belongs behind the character's feet and the near half in front, as do sparkles rising round it. A single image can only go wholly under the character or wholly over it, so give the parts behind a `depth`:
- On a layer: `front` (the default), `back`, or `split`, which cuts the layer at its centre row: the rows above go behind and the rest in front. Use `split` for a squashed ring or glyph lying round the feet; it cuts at the layer's current centre, so a ring rising round the body passes behind on its far side and in front on its near side.
- On an emitter: `front`, `back`, or `split`, which sends each particle behind when it is born above its spawn centre (sparkles rising from the far half of a circle) and in front otherwise. A child emitter without a `depth` of its own takes its parent particle's side.
- An effect with anything not in front also compiles `<effect>-back` and `<effect>-front`, with the same frames, timing, cues and anchor. The game draws `-back`, then its character, then `-front`. The whole `<effect>` is still compiled: it draws the back side under the front side, so the two parts drawn together give exactly its pixels.
- A sprite emitter that only draws can take `depth: "between"`: a stand-in character, drawn between the two sides in the whole effect and in neither part. The whole effect then reviews (and shares as a GIF) as the effect on a character, while the parts stay ready for the game's own.

Each side keeps the usual order within itself (behind particles, shapes, particles, front looks), but everything behind the character is under everything in front of it. A shape pixel goes to the side of the layer or blob that gave it the most; its outline and halo go with it, so the two sides meet without a seam. The holy and ice buffs in the spell library are built this way, with a plain stand-in figure.

Randomness is a seeded hash keyed by effect seed, emitter, spawn frame and particle, with a separate salt per property, so changing one range does not reshuffle the others, and the same source always compiles to the same recipe. A looping effect (`loop: true`, which needs a finite `life`) warms up for whole cycles with spawns keyed by their frame in the cycle, so the particles in flight at frame 0 are exactly those alive at the end. Particles partly off the canvas are cropped into grids and trails keep only on-canvas pixels, so compiled effects never clip. Metadata reports spawned particles, the most alive at once, operations and culled particles per effect, and the cues. It also reviews each animation: a loop's `seam` and a one-shot's `residue` (the pixels its last frame still draws, 0 when it ends empty). The compile draws the frame that should follow a loop's last, the next cycle's first, and `seam.jump` counts the pixels where the loop's own first frame differs from it: 0 for a loop with no seam, and anything else is warned about under `warnings`, naming the effect. `seam.pixels` (the pixels that change from the last frame to the first) and `largestStep` (the most between two other frames) are there too, but a seamless loop can have its biggest change at the seam by chance, so they are only a guide. Particles loop exactly by construction; what can jump is a layer's keys, spin or drift, a bolt `reroll` that doesn't divide the frames, or `flicker` over an odd number. Some keys can't show in that frame, so the compile also warns about them by name. These are a stream's `head` and `tail` keys and a `reveal` on a layer spanning the loop, and an emitter spanning the loop whose `path`, `sweep`, `throttle`, keyed `rate` or ring `grow` doesn't end where it starts. `edges` lists the canvas sides an animation draws on (its outermost row or column), each with the most pixels it draws there in one frame and the first and last frames that do. Whatever reaches past a side is cut off, so the compile warns about a side unless the effect lists it in its own `edges`: `["top"]` for a bolt from the sky, `["bottom"]` for a figure standing on the canvas's bottom row. A turned copy turns its sides with it. Another `seed` can throw a particle further than the reviewed one, so check the warnings again after reseeding (the Effects lab shows them under the preview). Each animation also gets a `light` track, one entry per frame: `at`, where its bright pixels are, weighted by brightness; `glow`, how much light they make, in full-white pixels; and `color`, their mean colour. An entry is null where nothing bright is drawn. Game code can drive a light from it: a flash on the impact frame, a glow that follows a projectile. With `light: true` on an effect, the track is also written into its frames as a point named `light`, on the frames that make light. A scene light with `follow: { "instance": "boom", "point": "light" }` then rides the effect's light, lights the normal-mapped sprites around it and goes dark when the effect does; key its `strength` to shape the flash. Atlases export the point, so a game's own light can do the same.

The compiler makes behaviour cheap to try; it does not supply it. Give each material its own motion and shapes (fire rises and fades, stone chips arc and settle, leaves flutter), keep particle counts low enough to read at native size, and review playback. An impact reads when it has one strong shape before the scatter: a white flash for a frame or two, then a ring or a fan of streaks that cool as they slow, a few secondary bits where they land, and smoke behind, fading by shrinking (a symbol sequence over the life) or cooling (`tint`) rather than by dither speckle. Keep fire below its source and debris on its floor. [examples/effects.fx.json](../examples/effects.fx.json) holds sparks, looping embers, landing dust, falling leaves and stone shards.

**Spell library.** `examples/<element>-spells.fx.json` holds a reviewed set of game spells for seven elements: fire, ice, lightning, water, poison, holy and shadow. Each file has the same four effects on one 64 × 64 canvas:
- `projectile`: the spell in flight, a seamless 8-frame loop turned to eight directions (`projectile-0` … `projectile-315`).
- `impact`: the hit, with a `hit` cue on the frame it lands.
- `area`: a ground effect, with a `hit` cue at its onset.
- `buff`: a seamless loop round a character about 16 × 34 standing there. The holy and ice buffs also compile `buff-back` and `buff-front` for the game to draw under and over its character, and draw a plain stand-in figure between them in `buff` itself; the other buffs draw no character.

Every effect is placed by the same point, the recipe's `anchor` at [32, 40]: a projectile's centre, an impact's hit point, an area's or a buff's spot on the ground. So a game can drop any of the 28 effects the same way. Each element moves like its material:
- Fire flickers, cools and turns to lit smoke.
- Ice is faceted and shatters into face-directed shards.
- Lightning forks, re-flashes and scorches.
- Water is cel-shaded and splashes into drops and ripples.
- Poison splats and leaves green gas.
- Holy is rays, halos and a glyph with a pillar of light.
- Shadow implodes, opens a void pool and grows tendrils.

Start from the nearest one and change only what is needed: a palette for a different tint, `turn` angles for fewer directions, timing for a slower cast. `test/spell-library.test.js` keeps every loop seamless and every one-shot ending empty.
## Fluid effects

A `pixelforge-fluid` source ([fluid.schema.json](../fluid.schema.json)) runs a real fluid simulation and compiles it into an ordinary recipe: every frame becomes one palette `grid` operation (plus `stamp`s for obstacles, or a `copy` of the part on the canvas of one that hangs off it), so inspect, patch, overlay and export work unchanged, and a colour outside the palette cannot appear. Use it where particles fall short: fire that rises and licks, smoke that curls and thins, steam, explosions, magic swirls, splashing and falling water, a bubbling potion. The grid has one cell per canvas pixel, up to 128 × 128.

```json
{ "format": "pixelforge-fluid", "version": 1, "name": "torch", "width": 16, "height": 32,
  "palette": { "r": "#c23b2e", "o": "#f57f2a", "y": "#ffd257", "W": "#fff7d6", "s": "#4a4150", "b": "#5c3a24", "k": "#221a1c" },
  "symbols": { "handle": [".kbbk.", ".kbbk.", ".kbbk.", "..kk.."] },
  "effects": { "torch": { "frames": 16, "duration": 70, "loop": true, "seed": 4, "buoyancy": 0.5, "cooling": 0.2,

    "emitters": [{ "at": [8, 25], "area": [5, 2], "rate": 1, "temperature": 1, "density": 0.25, "velocity": [0, -1],
      "noise": 0.8, "flicker": 0.5, "sparks": { "count": 0.15, "life": [6, 10], "colors": ["y", "o", "r"] } }],
    "look": [
      { "field": "density", "colors": ["s"], "thresholds": [0.2], "dither": 0.8 },
```

MCP `pixel_compile` takes the same source (`pixel_help` with `topic: "fluid"` returns the schema). The studio's **Fluid lab** (`/fluid.html`, linked from the studio) runs the same compiler in the browser: pick a preset, move a few sliders and download the GIF, sprite sheet, recipe or edited source; it shows the matching command line and MCP calls.

**Gases** (`kind: "gas"`, the default) use stable fluids on a staggered grid: semi-Lagrangian advection, a pressure solve with a fixed number of sweeps (`iterations`), vorticity confinement (`vorticity`, which keeps the small swirls that make smoke curl), buoyancy from temperature (`buoyancy`; `weight` makes dense smoke sink), `dissipation` of density, `cooling` of temperature, `drag` and `wind`. `advection: "sharp"` swaps the plain back-trace, which blurs a little every step, for MacCormack advection with a limiter: puffs keep a dense core and crisp edges and small swirls survive longer, at up to twice the cost and with no new peaks. Because it blurs less smoke away, a one-shot gas needs more `dissipation` to end empty: the explosion preset with sharp advection still has a few pixels on its last frame. **Liquids** (`kind: "liquid"`) carry water as FLIP particles (four per full cell) on the same grid, with a pressure solve by conjugate gradients (a third as many steps as `iterations`, each about three sweeps' work) that carries pressure the length of a pipe, a channel or a tall column within a step, so liquid standing in one comes to rest instead of sloshing, with `gravity`, `flip` (lively and noisier toward 1, calmer toward 0), `tension` (cohesion that keeps drops and streams together), `adhesion` and `drag`; `fill` places water at frame 0 in discs (`at` and `radius`), ellipses (`radius: [x, y]`), boxes (`x`, `y`, `w`, `h`) or their overlap, up to 64 shapes an effect: an ellipse cut by a box is a dome. `apic: true` makes the calm share of each particle's velocity affine (APIC): it also carries the turning and shearing of the flow around the particle, so a calm liquid keeps its swirls and folds instead of smearing them. `walls` closes canvas edges (open edges let gas and water out), and `obstacles` stamp symbols whose visible pixels block the flow (step by step, or along a smooth outline: see smooth obstacles below). The fluid never enters or crosses them, however fast it moves, even where a wall is one pixel thick: water slides along an obstacle, and a spark, droplet or bubble that runs into one ends there (a bubble bursts only where it reaches open air), except a bubble with `rise`, which slides along it (see sparks below); `draw` sets whether the obstacle is stamped in front of the fluid's outline and sparks (`over`, the default), behind them (`under`), or not at all (`none`, an invisible wall). `keys` on an obstacle move it or take it away on cue, for pieces that turn on a release: `keys: [{ frame: 15, show: false }]` is a dam that vanishes at frame 15, `[{ frame: 15, y: 0, over: 10 }]` a gate that lifts over ten frames, `[{ frame: 5, x: 40, over: 30 }]` a plunger. Each key starts from where the obstacle is on its frame and moves it in whole pixels, and `show: true` brings a hidden obstacle back. An obstacle, and any key, may put it partly or wholly off the canvas, up to its own size past an edge: what hangs off is clipped, as the canvas edge clips the fluid, so a press's rod can reach down from above the board, a gate can slide in from outside and a conveyor's load can leave (one that is off the canvas whenever it shows is pointed out under `warnings`). While a key's `over` runs, the obstacle pushes the fluid ahead of it: its faces carry its speed along the ramp, so the pressure solve sends the flow on steadily, and liquid or smoke in the cells it now covers is shoved along to the first open cell, never through another solid. A key with no `over` is a cut, not a move: the obstacle is simply somewhere else, it pushes nothing, and fluid where it lands is taken away, as it is where an obstacle appears or pushes fluid into a corner with nowhere to go. It is stamped at its place on each frame, and a loop repeats its keys every cycle (the place it comes round to is a cut too). `solid: false` makes an obstacle a decoration: it is drawn over or under the fluid as `draw` says, but the fluid never meets it, so a net, a candle flame, a label or a background needs no painting after the fact.

**Thick fluids.** `viscosity` (square pixels per frame, 0–64) is an implicit viscosity solve in stress form: it slows shearing and stretching but never motion as a whole, so a drop of honey falls exactly as fast as a drop of water, a stream stays one unbroken rope instead of breaking into drops, and a heap spreads slowly instead of splashing. For liquids, about 0.5 is oil, 2–4 syrup, 8–16 honey and 32–64 tar; for gases, 0.1–1 gives slow, smooth flows (above 1 a swirl dies within a few frames). It is not `drag`: drag takes a share of all motion away, falling included, as if the fluid moved through something thick, so a high drag makes everything slow, a falling drop included, but no thicker: it still spreads thin and breaks up. Walls and obstacles hold a viscous fluid, so honey runs slowly down a jar's side; `slip: true` lets it slide along them instead (a greased pan). A viscous liquid also calms its own particle jitter, so its effective FLIP share falls as it thickens; give it `apic: true` so that calm share keeps its folds. `yield` (liquids, 0–16) is a yield stress, for mud, slime, dough and toothpaste: below it the liquid is elastic, so a blob squashes where it lands and springs back, and a heap keeps its shape; pushed past it, the liquid flows (and with `viscosity` as well, flows thickly). A heap *h* pixels high holds itself up when `yield` is more than about gravity × *h* / 2, so at the default gravity of 0.3 a yield of 2 holds a 12-pixel heap and 0.5 lets it slump. `adhesion` (liquids, 0–4) is how strongly liquid holds on to walls and obstacles: liquid touching a solid stays on it unless pulled away faster than about `adhesion` ÷ 2 pixels per frame, so a film clings under a ledge or to a spoon while the rest falls away. Studio pieces turn on one decisive moment (a tear, a topple, a let-go), and a yield-stress heap left alone only creeps toward rest, so `keys` (liquids) change `yield`, `viscosity`, `adhesion`, `gravity` or `drag` at a chosen frame: `keys: [{ frame: 40, yield: 0.3 }]` holds a heap up for 40 frames, then lets it slump at once (a 21-pixel heap is 12 pixels high ten frames later), and `{ frame: 30, adhesion: 0 }` drops a blob that adhesion held under a ledge. `over` moves to the new values in a straight line over that many frames instead. To make a one-shot end at rest, key `drag` rather than `viscosity`: drag slows all motion alike and lets the water level as it calms, where thickening holds whatever shape it has, so a slosh freezes tilted. The dam-break example brings drag in while its wave crosses the tank (`{ frame: 16, drag: 0.07, over: 8 }`). Each setting starts from the effect's own value and follows the keys that name it, in frame order (a key that starts during another's `over` carries on from where that one had got to); a looping effect repeats them every cycle, and whitewater without its own `gravity` follows the keyed gravity. A viscous fluid costs about three times as much per step (a viscosity solve with as many conjugate-gradient steps as `iterations`, and for a liquid a second pressure solve), and a yield-stress liquid about four times (one more pressure solve, for the spreading that evens out its particles without straining it); lower `iterations` to 20–30 if a thick preset is slow.

**Sticky liquids.** A liquid moves with an obstacle it touches. While an obstacle key's `over` runs, adhesion holds liquid on the moving obstacle as it does on a still one, measured from where the obstacle really is along its move (it is drawn and solid in whole pixels, jumping a pixel at a time, but clinging follows it between the jumps), so a spoon lifted out of honey keeps a coat and draws the honey under it up after it. A viscous liquid is dragged along its sides unless it has `slip`. The cells it leaves stay liquid for a while (24 frames) wherever liquid or solid closes them in, so liquid flows in behind it instead of leaving a hole that a thick liquid would take many frames to close; where air reaches them, the obstacle has left the liquid and they are air. A cut (a key with no `over`) carries nothing. Clinging has a speed limit, the `adhesion` ÷ 2 pixels per frame above, so an obstacle moving faster than that sheds what clings to it: lift a spoon at about a pixel a frame with `adhesion` 2–4. An obstacle's own `adhesion` (0–4) replaces the effect's for liquid touching it, so a sticky spoon (`adhesion: 4` on the spoon) can stand in a non-stick pan (the effect's `adhesion` 0) or a non-stick spoon (`adhesion: 0`) in a jar the honey clings to. Honey pulled between a spoon and a pool stretches into a string one particle wide, which the density spreads over three pixels and loses long before it breaks. A `strand` look layer draws it: the field is 1 along a one-pixel path through the cells that hold particles but too little density, where that path joins two separate pieces of drawn liquid or hangs at least 3 pixels off one (what is left of a string that has snapped). Nubs and fringes on a surface and particles loose in the air are not strands, so a surface gains no bumps or stray pixels. Put it after `light` and `shadow`, which would recolour it, in a pale colour for a glossy thread. The string breaks where a cell of it empties: cohesion (`tension`) pulls it into the drops at its ends sooner, and viscosity holds it together longer. The [honey dipper](../examples/honey-dipper.fluid.json) lifts a sticky dipper out of a nearly non-stick jar of honey.

**Electricity and plasma.** A gas can carry electricity. An obstacle with a `voltage` is an electrode: a number of volts, or keys `[{ frame, voltage, over }]` that change it on cue (strikes, pulses), repeated every cycle in a loop. Arcs run between electrodes at different voltages, through the gas and through `conductor` obstacles (metal at no set voltage), never through other obstacles: one electrode facing a plain obstacle, or floating metal, has no field and nothing ionizes. A decoration with a voltage (`solid: false`) lets the gas through and may be unseen (`draw: "none"`), such as the charge inside a cloud. Each step solves the electric field over the gas (∇·(σ∇φ) = 0 by conjugate gradients, `electric.iterations` steps) and grows plasma by the dielectric breakdown model: `growth` cells a frame ionize beside an electrode, metal or a growing leader, drawn one at a time by seeded chance among the cells whose field exceeds `breakdown` (volts per pixel, lower in hot gas) in proportion to (field ÷ breakdown)^`focus` − 1, so `focus` about 1 forks freely like lightning and 3–4 keeps to one path like an arc between close plates. Plasma rides the gas as a chain of linked points drawn one pixel thin, so heat lifts and bends an arc without blurring it, and an arc's ends slide along the metal. A leader stays lit while it grows; once it joins two electrodes, current flows: current at or above `sustain` keeps a channel fully ionized, below half of it a channel goes out, and a burning arc grows no branches. Without current, plasma recombines at `recombine` a frame (more slowly when hot), fading through the look's bands, and leaves a trace for `memory` frames that the next strike runs down at once, as a lightning restrike does (`memory: 0` for none, as a Jacob's ladder wants). Current heats the gas by `heating` × current² a frame, so buoyancy lifts an arc. An electrode's `resistance` is its supply's own: its voltage sags as current flows (current ≈ voltage ÷ (resistance + arc length ÷ 8)), so a long arc starves and a shorter path takes over, and one strike stops the other leaders; give an arc enough resistance that it carries a few times `sustain`, or it grows into a net of parallel paths. `charge` makes an electrode store charge (current × frames per volt) that strikes drain and its supply refills through its resistance, so strikes come in bursts. `electric.ground` holds canvas edges at 0 V: arcs that reach them strike them, but they grow none. Gas hotter than `electric.ionize` is plasma as well, fully ionized at half as hot again, with or without electrodes: an arc's hot air, a fireball's core. It is 2 by default, above every flame on the shared heat scale (flames run 0.3 to 1), so fire never ionizes by accident; 0 turns it off. Look layers draw `ionization` (0–1: 1 in a live channel, fading as it recombines) and `current` (the current through each pixel; the metadata's `peak.current` gives the brightest), and `halo` on any layer draws a glow round what it drew. Defaults (they apply when only an obstacle has a voltage): breakdown 3, focus 2, growth 8, recombine 0.3, sustain 0.5, memory 8, heating 0.2, ionize 2, no ground, iterations 24. A gas with electrodes needs no emitter (`emitters: []`), at most 8 obstacles may have a voltage, and an electric gas costs about two to three times plain gas per step (a field solve for each electrode with a resistance, and one for the ideal ones when any is above 0 V). In a loop, `blend: 1` keeps one strike from ghosting over the next, and switching the voltage off for the cycle's last frames starts every cycle afresh.

A liquid with `heat` and `vapour` (see phase changes below) carries electricity too, with the same settings: arcs burn in the air over it, the vapour's gas, whose flow lifts and bends them, and the field is solved through the water and ice as well. Water conducts (moderately: an arc's current spreads through a pool) and takes an arc that reaches it as a grounded edge does, so a leader that comes near the water strikes it and its foot stays where it struck, but the water grows no streamers of its own; ice hardly conducts, and plasma never enters either. The current through the water heats every particle there, as current² ÷ conductivity heats anything: `heating` × current² × 16 a frame in water, which conducts a sixteenth as well as a full channel. The current spreads out as soon as it enters a pool, so the water at the arc's foot boils and steams with no setting of its own, and the steam leaves as any steam does. Over a liquid `electric.iterations` defaults to 48, since the water makes the field slower to solve. Draw the plasma in `vapour.look` (`ionization` and `current` there; the liquid's own look draws the water). Ground the water through a grounded edge it touches (`electric.ground`) or an electrode at 0 V under it, or it floats and an arc that lands on it goes out. The lightning-pond preset strikes a pond twice.

**Smooth obstacles.** Obstacles are whole pixels, so a slope drawn in pixel art is a staircase, and a thick liquid catches on every step: wet snow that slides down an upright wall stalls on a 45° roof, and slime pressed against the underside of a stepped branch hangs from it. `surface: "smooth"` on an obstacle makes the fluid meet an outline traced through its steps instead. Each corner that sticks out loses a triangle with legs of half a pixel and each corner that turns in gains one, so a 1:1 staircase acts as a straight 45° slope, a 1:2 staircase as alternating 45° and upright runs, and straight edges stay where the pixels are. The faces along the outline are partly open in the pressure and viscosity solves, particles slide along the outline keeping their speed, and a smooth obstacle pushes on a liquid but never pulls it, so a fill against its underside falls away and a springy blob can bounce off it (`adhesion` still holds liquid on). With `slip`, snow and slime that stall on a stepped roof slide off a smooth one; without it, the outline drags on a viscous liquid as a wall does, so honey only creeps down it (a springy blob can still bounce down). The obstacle is drawn as it is, pixel for pixel. A line one pixel thick is smoothed along its upper side only, so draw slopes at least two pixels thick to smooth both sides; nothing is smoothed where a smooth obstacle meets a pixel one or the canvas edge. Pixel obstacles, the default, behave exactly as before.

An emitter is a disc (`radius`, default 2) or a box (`area`) centred on `at`. A `burst` adds its amounts once, at `start`; a `rate` emitter works every frame until `end`, holding its cells at the amounts rather than piling them up, so a steady source keeps its level (a short window such as `end: 3` gives a blast that keeps growing for a few frames). Each is a strength multiplier. Gas emitters add or hold `density` (smoke) and `temperature` (heat, which rises); liquid emitters fill their cells with water (`density` is the share filled, default 1) or drain them (a negative share of the particles there each frame, which balances a source in a loop). `velocity` steers the flow (a jet; `[0, 0]` holds the covered cells still, as a drop gathering at a tip), `vortex` swirls it (clockwise for positive values), and `expand` pushes outward: a gas expands through the pressure solve, as hot gas does. `noise` adds coherent turbulence, `flicker` varies the strength from cell to cell and frame to frame so a flame's base breaks into tongues, `pulse` makes the strength rise and fall (puffs, heaves) and `orbit` moves the emitter in a circle, or an ellipse with `radius: [x, y]`. An orbiting rate emitter is laid along the path it covered since the last frame, so a fast orbit leaves a smooth trail. `falloff` (0–1) makes the amounts fall off from the centre toward the rim, so one emitter makes a round puff that is densest in the middle and shrinks as a ball when it thins. `sparks` are small particles carried by the flow and drawn over it, with their own `colors` over their life, `size` (1–3 pixels; 2 reads at 1×) and an optional `trail` that streaks back to where the spark was a frame earlier: embers, sparkles, debris (`follow: 0` ignores the flow; in a liquid such spray ends where it falls back into the water, flying on past its `life` if need be) and bubbles (`pop: true` bursts them where they leave the liquid: a ring for one frame, then two droplets). In a liquid, `rise` makes a spark a bubble that behaves like air: it rises `rise` pixels per frame through still water (about 0.3–0.8), against the liquid's gravity and on top of the flow that carries it (`follow`), so a current sweeps it along and a plunging stream drags it down before it rises. It never ends at an obstacle or a closed edge: it slides along whatever stops it (and a solid that moves onto it pushes it aside), up a sloping ceiling and out from under a ledge within five pixels, or waits under it, and bubbles waiting under the same ceiling that touch merge into one holding the air of both, up to three pixels across. It ends where it reaches the air, bursting there with `pop`, or at the end of its life. `wobble` sways it that many pixels from side to side as it rises, and `lift` (0–1) hands that share of its buoyancy to the water around it, as if that much of the water weighed nothing: with about a bubble a frame, `lift: 1` raises a low dome on the surface over the column, as an aerator does, and 0.3 a slight bump. An emitter with no amounts of its own is a handy spark source, such as embers rising above a flame rather than inside the logs. A puff of gas can't be thrown: the still air has to get out of its way, so the pressure solve halves its speed at once, and what is left blurs away within a few pixels. Dust can be thrown, because dust is grains. A gas emitter with `carry` puts its smoke and heat on carriers instead (it needs a positive `density` or `temperature`, and it does not push the air itself), one per covered cell (every frame, for a rate emitter, which then throws its amounts each frame; an orbiting one throws once a frame), launched with its `velocity`, `vortex`, `expand` (outward) and `noise`. Each carrier loses about `carry.drag` (default 0.1) of the difference between its velocity and the air's each frame, falls by its own `carry.gravity`, hands `carry.release` (default 0.2) of what it still holds to the air each frame, and drops the rest where it meets an obstacle or a closed edge, so dust settles on a closed floor; through an open edge it leaves the canvas. What it holds is drawn with the smoke. Two bursts thrown sideways at 8 pixels a frame beside an impact roll out as a dust wave about 90 pixels across within six frames, where plain gas emitters stay about 26 pixels across: dust waves, smoke from a skidding wheel, dash trails.

`look` turns fields into pixels, one layer after another. Each layer names a `field` and gives palette `colors` with rising `thresholds`: a colour is fully on from its threshold up, and `dither` (0–1, default 0.5) feathers it in below through an ordered `pattern` anchored to the canvas (bayer4 by default; the same patterns as the `dither` operation). Fields are `density`, `temperature` (gases), `speed` (pixels per frame: foam, fast water; a liquid has speed wherever there is at least a quarter of a cell of water, so a speed layer also draws thin, fast water such as a falling drop; a gas has speed wherever its air moves, smoke or not, so a gas speed layer also draws moving clear air), `depth` (pixels down from a liquid's surface, 1 on its top row, for a highlight line and deeper bands, counted through every pixel the liquid's own layers draw, so thin fast water drawn by `speed` doesn't start a new surface under it; from the third row down it follows the shallowest surface within three pixels along the row, so the bands stay level under a landing drop or a rising crest), and `light` and `shadow` (0–1: how sharply density falls toward or away from the `light` direction, upper left by default), which only recolour pixels earlier layers drew and give smoke and fireballs a lit form, and `strand` (liquids: 1 along a one-pixel string of liquid stretched thinner than the density can draw; see sticky liquids above); electric gases add `ionization` and `current` (see electricity above). A layer's `halo: { color, width }` (width 1–4, default 1) draws a glow of that colour on the empty pixels within `width` of what the layer drew, after cleanup: an arc's glow, a rim round a flame. A liquid touching an obstacle on the side toward the light (or away from it) is lit (or shaded) in full there, and obstacles further off take no part, so a pool in a stepped bowl gets an even rim along it. Colours are one-character palette keys, since grid rows name colours by character. A liquid's `density` counts only the cells water can be in: an obstacle holds none and thins none of the water beside it, so water in a crack one pixel wide or a notch walled in on three sides shows as fully as open water. Before any of that, a liquid's outline is tidied: a gap one pixel wide with liquid on two sides and liquid or an obstacle on a third is filled, and a lone pixel standing on a straight edge is trimmed, so a resting surface keeps no dents or pimples that flicker from frame to frame (a lone drop, a stream's tip and a two-pixel step stay). `cleanup` runs De-Stray and De-Corner on the fluid (De-Stray never erases a liquid's lone pixel, which is a drop whose water is there, and De-Corner leaves a pixel tucked against an obstacle alone, so it never opens a gap between the fluid and a wall), then `outline` draws a one-pixel outline around it and sparks are drawn over it.

`whitewater` (liquids, up to four layers) throws spray and foam off the water by its own motion, so a splash or a waterfall needs no spray emitters timed to the frame where the water lands. A layer picks particles at the surface by `impact` (its velocity changed by at least that many pixels per frame within one frame: a drop landing, water striking water) or by `speed` (it moves at least that fast: a stream shedding drops). Each throws a droplet with probability `chance` per frame, along that change or that motion tipped away from the water, at `throw` times the amount and turned by up to `spread` degrees. Droplets are sparks with their own `colors`, `life`, `size`, `trail`, `gravity` (by default the effect's) and `drag`. With `follow: 0` (the default) they are spray: drawn once they have left the water, and ending where they fall back in. A droplet still in the air when its `life` runs out flies on, for up to its life again, until it lands, hits an obstacle or leaves the canvas, so spray never vanishes in mid-air. With `follow` above 0 they are foam carried on the flow, ending once the flow carries them under. A layer with `rise` makes bubbles instead, air the water drives under its own surface: it picks particles in the body of the water, two pixels from air every way (so never in a falling stream a few pixels thick), by `speed` (a plunging stream driving into a pool) or `impact` (a drop landing in it), and leaves a bubble at rest there that rides the flow (`follow` defaults to 1), rises and ends at the surface as a spark with `rise` does, with `wobble`, `lift` and `pop` as for sparks (`throw`, `spread` and `gravity` shape spray only). Keep `chance` low (a few hundredths): every fast particle under a plunge qualifies. The splash and waterfall presets throw spray and foam; the aquarium preset makes bubbles.

**Air pockets.** No air is simulated by default: air the liquid seals off has no pressure, so an upturned cup pushed into water floods at once and a sealed bottle pours out in one go. `air` (liquids) keeps it. With `air: { stiffness: 40 }`, a region of air sealed off from the open canvas edges is a pocket that keeps its amount of air, and its pressure follows the gas law: squeezed, it pushes back; stretched, it pulls. `stiffness` (2–1000, default 40) is the head, in pixels of liquid, that squeezes a pocket to half its size: under *h* pixels of liquid a pocket keeps stiffness ÷ (stiffness + *h*) of its size, so at 40 an upturned cup fifteen pixels down keeps about three quarters of its air, and at 1000 it barely gives. Each pocket's pressure is solved with the liquid's, so any stiffness is stable at any `substeps`. Pockets that meet merge their air, and one that splits shares it out. So air stays under a shelf, a big bubble rises through the water and joins the air above it, and an upside-down bottle glugs: the space it leaves at the top stretches and holds the rest back until outside air pushes up the neck as a bubble, then it pours again. A gap of fewer than three cells between jostling particles is not a pocket, and closes as before. A pocket squeezed below three cells, which the surface's blur would fill in anyway, lets its air go: `air.bubbles` (`colors`, and `life`, `rise`, `wobble`, `lift` and `pop` as for bubbles) draws that air as bubbles as large as the air they hold; without it the air leaves unseen. The same goes for a sealed gap of three to eleven cells with no pocket before it, air a splash or a pour has trapped: it rises as bubbles from the liquid at its edge and the gap closes, so trapped air fizzes up instead of standing in the water as holes; from twelve cells it is a pocket, and rises through the water as one. A bubble that rises into a pocket gives it its air, so the glugging bottle's bubbles add to the air at its top. Bubbles waiting under a ceiling become a pocket of their own once there is enough of their air: two that touch but cannot merge (a bubble holds at most twelve square pixels and must fit where it waits, so under a flat ceiling bubbles stop growing at six), holding eight square pixels of air or more between them, clear that many cells under the ceiling (at least twelve), the water there gives way downward and rises elsewhere, and the pocket keeps their air; the bubbles that follow join it, so air bubbled under an upturned cup gathers into a growing pocket and lowers the water inside. A boil's bubbles, which carry vapour, stay bubbles. A pocket whose air reaches open air vents: its pressure is gone, so a cup held mouth down with its top out of the water keeps its air until a hole opens in its top, then the water gulps up inside it. A body of liquid sealed in on every side by obstacles and ice, with no air at all, keeps its volume. It is off by default because it changes every sealed space: a mould filling from above traps air in its tips (filling them is what most studio pieces want), and a closed tank's air pushes on its water. Air makes a liquid up to about a quarter slower to compile.

**Phase changes.** A liquid with `heat` carries heat in every particle and changes phase: ice melts and freezes, water boils into steam and condenses back, frost grows from vapour and ice can turn straight into vapour. Temperatures use the gas scale: 0 is the room's air (`ambient`, default 0), water freezes at `freeze` (default −0.5) and boils at `boil` (default 0.5), and flames run about 0.3 to 1, so a flame-hot obstacle boils water. Heat comes from fills (`temperature`, and `frozen: true` for an ice cube), from liquid emitters (`temperature`) and from obstacles with a `temperature` (a hot plate, a cold lid or window; without one an obstacle takes no part), and flows each step between neighbouring water and ice (`conduct`, default 0.12 a frame), from hot or cold obstacles (`contact`, 0.15) and between the surface and the air (`air`, 0.04); the air drifts back to `ambient`. Melting and boiling take time: at `freeze` the temperature holds while a particle takes up `latent` (default 0.5) and at `boil` while it takes up its `vaporize` (default 1, half to one and a half times that per particle, so a pot bubbles steadily), so ice never flickers at the threshold. Ice needs a seed: water below `freeze` turns to ice where it touches ice or a solid colder than freezing, or anywhere once it is 0.5 below, so a pond chilled from above stays open until the ice creeping from its cold shore reaches it (`grain`, 0–1, makes the front ragged). Ice holds still, a cell of it is solid to the water, to bubbles and to air pockets, and a piece that touches no obstacle and has nothing under it settles as one piece, floating where water flanks it: an ice cube settles into its own meltwater. `ice: { look }` draws the ice cell by cell over the water (density 0.5 is a cell of ice, with `light` and `shadow` rims); without it ice takes the water's look.

With `vapour`, water at the surface that has boiled becomes vapour, and `evaporation` (0–1) lets a share of warm surface water steam off below the boil. Vapour is a gas over the cells holding neither water nor ice, moved by the gas solver with the water and ice as walls moving with it: `expansion` (default 8) is how many cells a cell's worth of water fills as steam, which is how much room it takes on screen and how hard a boil pushes the air; `buoyancy` lifts vapour hotter than the air against gravity and `weight` sinks it by its density (negative for steam, positive for dry-ice fog); `vorticity`, `cooling`, `dissipation`, `drag` and `spread` (diffusion, for still humid air) work as for gases, and `vapour.look` draws it under the water with gas-style layers (density 1 is a cell full of steam). Boiling inside the water makes `vapour.bubbles` (bubble settings as for sparks with `rise`; with `lift` the boil churns the pot) that carry their vapour up and let it out at the surface, or into an air pocket, raising its pressure. Heat moves by temperature, which holds at the boil, so water that boils under the surface stays there unless bubbles carry it up: a pool heated from below by an obstacle hotter than `boil` steams only with `vapour.bubbles` or `evaporation`, and is warned about without either. Vapour touching anything colder than `dew` (default `boil` − 0.1) condenses, `condense` of it a frame: onto water it joins the water; on an obstacle or ice it gathers as dew, drawn as water, and drips off as a drop once a cell holds a cell's worth, so steam beads under a cold lid and drips back. With `frost` set (at or below `freeze`), vapour touching ice or an obstacle colder than `frost` grows straight into ice (`deposit` of it a frame), only from those cold surfaces and the frost already there, never in mid-air, and only into air below `freeze`. It grows as fern-like fingers a pixel wide: a finger starts at seeded spots on a cold surface, most often in its corners, where the cold is on two sides, grows straight out at its tip and forks at 45 degrees into shorter branches a pixel apart (`branch`, 0–1, default 0.5, is how often; 0 grows straight fingers), and stops short of other fingers, so a cold window frosts over in feathers rather than a rim. Under gravity frost stays where it grew: a finger's cells, which touch only at a corner where it grows at 45 degrees, hold together with the frost and the cold surface they grew from. `deposit` and `branch` need `frost`, and `condense` needs `vapour`. Open cold vapour never condenses into mist on its own: vapour cooled below the room's air already stops rising and sinks, and `vapour.look` draws it as fog. With `sublimate`, ice turns straight into vapour at its surface once it has taken up `latent` and `vaporize`, and its vapour comes back only as frost: dry ice. A `fill` with `vapour` holds still vapour of that density (humid air). Matter is kept exactly: each frame's figures carry `water` (dew included), `ice`, `vapour` (bubbles included), `added` (by emitters since the first frame) and `taken` (by drains, open edges and `dissipation`), all in cells' worth, and water + ice + vapour + taken − added stays what the fills held. Obstacles with heat may move on keys and be smooth: a hot spoon lifted out of honey carries a warm coat, a heated piston boils the water it pushes into, and an ice cube melting on a smooth hot slope runs down it. Ice meets the water pixel by pixel, beside smooth obstacles too. A liquid without `heat` compiles exactly as before; heat alone adds under a tenth, and vapour about doubles a frame's cost (a second pressure solve over the air). In a gas effect, an obstacle's `temperature` warms or chills the air touching it, so a hot plate sends up a plume.

A looping effect (`loop: true`) repeats its forcing every cycle: emitter timing, pulses, orbits, noise and flicker are keyed by the frame within the cycle, so pulse and orbit periods must divide the frame count. The simulation warms up for `warmup` whole cycles (default 2; raise it when a flow takes long to settle, such as a pool filling), then blends the fields (not the pixels) of its closing `blend` frames toward the frames that preceded frame 0, so the last frame flows into the first; sparks that outlive the cycle are drawn from the start again, so they loop exactly. So are a boil's bubbles: a loop draws those born in its recorded cycle, simulating on after it until they have ended, and those of its warm-up stay unseen. Bubbles that push the water, those with `lift` and, with air pockets on, every bubble (a pocket's own included), are made through the warm-up in the same way, so the dome an aerator raises and the pockets bubbles gather into are there from frame 0. A one-shot effect should end empty (gas) or at rest (liquid): the metadata's `residue` is the number of pixels left in the last frame, and `pixels` counts the pixels of every frame. Each effect's `figures` give, one list per figure with an entry per frame, what tuning by eye would otherwise have to judge: `box` (the fluid's drawn pixels as `[left, top, right, bottom]`, sparks left out, or null), `middle` (the middle of its density, or null), `amount` (the smoke there is, or cells' worth of water), `pieces` (how many separate pieces it is drawn in, pixels touching side to side), `speed` (its top speed where it is drawn, in pixels per frame) and, for a liquid, `particles`. A splash shows as the drop and the pool merging from 2 pieces into 1, a burst as its pieces, a slump as `box` widening while its top sinks, and a leak or a drain as `particles` falling. Limits keep compiles quick and bounded: at most 16 effects of up to 16 emitters and 32 obstacles, a 128 × 128 canvas, 32,768 liquid particles and 4,096 sparks alive at once, flows no faster than 32 pixels per frame, and about 400 million cell updates of simulation per source (the presets need at most about 230 million, the electric ones, and most under 90 million; a live runner counts only its warm-up); an error names the effect that goes over. Everything is deterministic: only `+ − × ÷` and square roots, the polynomial trigonometry and seeded hash in `craft.js`, a fixed timestep (`substeps` per frame) and fixed iteration counts, so the same source gives the same recipe on every machine and in the browser. Each example compiles in about a second or less: most in well under one, the electric and steam presets in up to about a second and a quarter.

Tuning tips. Fire needs strong `buoyancy` (0.4–0.5) and `cooling` (0.13–0.2) so the flame stays short, `noise` and `flicker` for tongues, and a temperature ramp from dark red to near-white with the white threshold above the source level so the core only flashes white. Smoke wants a dark-to-light ramp with generous `dither` so it thins rather than ending in a hard edge. Explosions are a short `rate` window with strong `temperature` and `expand`, then cooling into smoke; check them at 1× with `inspect --scale 1`. Liquids need closed `walls` or a basin, a body colour at density 0.5 and `depth` bands; give a looping liquid a way out (an open edge past a lip, or a draining emitter) so its level settles during the warm-up; a drain laid along the surface you want (a `seep`) keeps the level there whatever flows in. Honey wants `viscosity` 8–16, `apic: true` and a low `tension` (0.3), since cohesion pulls a thin rope into beads; draw the rope by `speed` as well as density, as the waterfall is drawn, and over the depth bands wherever it moves (a long column from its source would otherwise darken in the same bands as a deep pool); `light` and `shadow` layers give it gloss. Keep a looping stream's `pulse` gentle: a strong pulse makes the stream sway differently each cycle, and the blended closing frames then show two streams at once. Slime wants a high `yield` (12–16), so it stays elastic through its landing and bounces back, `apic: true`, four `substeps` (less damping, so it wobbles before it rests) and `tension` 2 to round it; light it with `light` and `shadow` layers rather than depth bands, which draw a dark wedge inside a ball. Inspect `--diagnostics` on looping results: the loop-boundary check stays quiet on every example.

Four things that catch people out, each of which `compileFluid` (and so `pixel_compile` and `pixelbolt compile`) reports under `warnings`, naming the field, and a live runner lists in `live.warnings`. An emitter whose cells lie inside an obstacle (or off the canvas) on every frame it works makes no fluid: the fluid never enters obstacles, so `peak` in the metadata stays at zero (and `pixels` too, unless the emitter has sparks, which still appear). Move the source just outside the obstacle's visible pixels. A liquid fill that starts against the underside of a pixel obstacle, or of a closed edge, with open space below it, hangs there: the pressure solve holds liquid on to the solid it starts against. Give the obstacle `surface: "smooth"`, or start the fill a pixel lower. A fill that rests on something along a quarter or more of its lowest cells, such as water in a flask or on another fill's water, is not warned about, whatever it touches; nor is one thrown away from what is above it, bursting, or in a liquid with `adhesion`, which is meant to cling. An obstacle hotter than `heat.boil` under a liquid with `vapour` but neither `vapour.bubbles` nor `heat.evaporation` heats water that waits at the boil under the surface, so the pool never steams: give it bubbles (see phase changes). And outward `velocity` given to one connected blob of liquid is largely cancelled by the pressure solve, since a liquid can't expand, so a ball thrown outward from its middle stays one ball. To burst a liquid in mid-air, give its fill `burst: { speed, pieces }`: the fill breaks into that many seeded pieces (8 by default) with a one-pixel gap between them, each flying straight out from the fill's middle at `speed` pixels per frame (a number, or `[min, max]` picked per piece) plus the fill's `velocity`. Each piece's corners are rounded off, so the pieces fly as blobs rather than shards, and the water of the gaps and corners goes to the pieces, so the volume is kept. For a splash, throw the water onto something instead: an obstacle (`draw: "none"` makes an invisible floor) or a pool of water.

### Live fluids

`createLiveFluid` (from `pixelbolt/fluid`, or `src/fluid.js` in a web page) runs one effect of a fluid source live, a frame at a time. A page can then answer the pointer on the next frame instead of compiling a whole loop again. It runs the same simulation as `compileFluid`: the same solver, emitters, sparks and whitewater, drawn with the effect's own look layers, cleanup and outline. The same source and the same calls give the same pixels in Node, Chromium, Firefox and WebKit.

```js
import { createLiveFluid } from 'pixelbolt/fluid';
const live = createLiveFluid(source, { effect: 'torch', warmup: 16 }); // checks the source as compileFluid does, then warms up
live.push({ x: 8, y: 20, vx: 2, vy: -1, radius: 5 });                  // stir: velocity in pixels per frame, soft disc
live.puff({ x: 8, y: 24, radius: 3, density: 1, temperature: 1 });     // more smoke and heat (in a liquid, water)
live.boost('flame', 3, 40);                                            // the emitter named flame, 3 × as strong for 40 frames
live.step();                                                           // the push and puff act in this frame
const { rgba, keys } = live.render();                                  // RGBA pixels, and each pixel's palette key
const { box, middle, amount, pieces } = live.measure();                // this frame's figures, as the metadata gives them
```

- **`createLiveFluid(source, { effect, warmup })`** makes every check `compileFluid` makes before simulating, with the same error paths and messages, except the work budget: a compile counts every frame of every effect, while a live run steps one frame at a time, so only its warm-up is held to the 400 million cell updates (an error at `live.warmup` says how many frames fit). The limits a compile meets while simulating (particles, sparks, the renderer's drawing budget) don't stop a live run. Its own options fail at `live.effect` and `live.warmup`. `effect` names the effect to run (default: the first). `warmup` is the number of frames simulated before it returns (default: one cycle, the effect's `frames`; at most 2,048). Inline the palette, since a page can't follow a palette `$ref`.
- **Properties:** `width`, `height`, `kind` (`gas` or `liquid`), `frames` (the cycle length) and `palette` (each key's colour as `#rrggbb`, or `#rrggbbaa` when translucent). Also, read-only: `frame` (frames simulated, warm-up included), `particles` and `sparks` (how many are alive).
- **`measure()`** returns the current frame's figures (`box`, `middle`, `amount`, `pieces`, `speed` and, for a liquid, `particles`), as a compiled effect's metadata gives them per frame.
- **`step(n = 1)`** simulates `n` frames (at most 2,048 a call), each in the effect's `substeps`. Emitter windows, pulses, orbits, noise and flicker follow the frame number modulo `frames`, as in a loop, so a looping effect keeps looping and a one-shot repeats every `frames` frames. There is no seam to blend, and sparks join on every frame.
- **`push({ x, y, vx, vy, radius })`** adds velocity in pixels per frame (−16 to 16) with a soft disc falloff (radius 4 by default). A gas gets it in its flow; a liquid gets it on the particles in range.
- **`puff({ x, y, radius, density, temperature })`** in a gas adds density and heat (−16 to 16; default 1 and 0) with the same soft falloff; negative amounts take them away. In a liquid it fills the disc's cells to `density`, the share filled (up to 1, default 1), with still water, as a liquid emitter's disc does; a negative share (down to −1) drains that share of the particles there.
- Pushes and puffs act at the next step, after the emitters and in the order they were made. A push's change of speed doesn't count as a whitewater impact, though the water it throws can land as one. At most 1,024 wait for a step; beyond that the oldest are dropped, so stop queueing while stepping is paused.
- **`boost(name, strength, frames)`** multiplies the strength (the `rate` or `burst`) of every emitter with that `name` by `strength` (0–16; 0 turns it off) for the next `frames` frames. A later boost of the same emitter replaces it. Strength scales noise and the number of sparks thrown. In a gas it also scales expansion, a burst's amounts and the kick of its `velocity`, and the density and temperature a rate emitter holds. A rate emitter steers its cells toward its `velocity` (a liquid one also toward its `expand`) more firmly but no faster. A liquid emitter fills its disc to its `density` share at most, so at a rate of 1 or more boosting it adds little water, and only water already in a liquid burst's disc gets the stronger kick. Push for a stronger jet, or puff for more water. Outside its `start`–`end` window, a boosted emitter stays idle.
- **`render()`** returns `{ rgba, keys, fluid }`. `rgba` is a `Uint8ClampedArray` of width × height × 4 values, ready for `new ImageData(rgba, width, height)`. `keys` has one string per row naming the topmost palette key drawn at each pixel (`.` where nothing is drawn), so a page can recolour or overlay by key. `fluid` has one string per row with `#` where the look layers drew the fluid itself (after cleanup; under any spark, whitewater or decoration drawn over it) and `.` elsewhere, so a page or an agent can tell the fluid from an outline, a spark or a decoration drawn with the same key: count a pocket's liquid from `fluid`, not from `keys`. With an opaque palette, each pixel is exactly its key's colour; a translucent colour blends over whatever is drawn beneath it, so there the pixel is a mix and the key names only its top layer. It draws the look layers, cleanup and outline, then sparks and whitewater, then the obstacles stamped under or over, exactly as a compiled frame does. A one-shot effect run from `warmup: 0` reproduces `compileFluid`'s frames. A loop with no sparks or whitewater, warmed up for the compiler's cycles, matches its frames until the seam blend. Sparks and whitewater can differ: the compiler adds them during the recorded cycle only (bubbles that push the water from its warm-up on, drawing those born in the cycle) and wraps the late ones round to frame 0, while a live run adds them on every frame.
- `step`, `push`, `puff` and `boost` return the runner, so calls chain: `live.push(…).step().render()`. Once running, a live run never stops at a limit: beyond 32,768 particles or 4,096 sparks, more are not added (a liquid's starting `fill` is checked as `compileFluid` checks it). A push or puff never fails on a number out of range: `x` and `y` are clamped to −256 to 512, `radius` to 0.5 to 64, and `vx`, `vy`, `density` and `temperature` to the ranges above. A missing `x` or `y`, or a value that is not a number (`NaN` included), fails with a path such as `push.x`, so guard pointer maths such as a distance over a zero time. `boost` and `step` check their arguments strictly (`boost.name`, `step`).

One step costs about what one compiled frame does. On a desktop CPU, the portfolio hero's 128 × 60 steam (30 pressure sweeps, 2 substeps) steps in about 7–9 ms in Node, Chromium, Firefox and WebKit, and renders in about 1 ms; its 44 × 50 liquid flask steps in about 2 ms. A step's time grows with `substeps` (up to 16): the 48 × 64 waterfall steps in about 2 ms at 2, 7 ms at 8 and 17 ms at 16. Creating a runner simulates its warm-up, about 0.7 s for that steam's 96 frames. So create runners after the first paint, or in a Web Worker that sends `rgba` to the page (transfer its buffer). Stepping at the effect's own frame duration (70 ms is typical) keeps the look of the compiled loop.

## Lossless raster return path

```sh
node bin/pixelbolt.js import corrected.png --name refined --atlas optional.atlas.json --out refined.json --metadata import.provenance.json
```

Supported PNGs are non-interlaced with up to 8 bits per channel, in every colour type: RGB, RGBA, greyscale, greyscale with alpha and indexed (1, 2, 4 or 8 bits per pixel), with `tRNS` transparency (an alpha for each palette entry, or one key colour), standard filters and checked chunk CRCs, at most 32 MiB compressed, 4096 per side and 16,777,216 pixels. APNG, 16-bit and interlaced PNGs and unsupported critical chunks fail clearly. No quantization, resizing, alpha modification or color-profile conversion is performed: palette entries and their alpha are copied as stored, and greyscale below 8 bits takes the 8-bit value the PNG specification gives it (4-bit `7` is `#777777`). A greyscale key colour below 8 bits matches the stored sample, as libpng and Godot read it; Pillow and ffmpeg ignore such a key. `provenance.encoding` names the colour type, bit depth and whether `tRNS` was present. A single image must fit the sprite limits. Optional Pixelbolt/TexturePacker-style metadata requires `meta.scale:1`, equal-size untrimmed/unrotated rectangles, and supported frame counts/source-area limits; durations and expanded named sequences/loop flags survive. Inputs with at most 256 visible colors (the palette limit) return as compact palette grids, using ASCII keys first and then single-code-unit Latin letters; richer inputs use exact final `pixels`. Hidden RGB under alpha zero is preserved with explicit corrections when the PNG has an alpha channel (RGBA, greyscale with alpha). Under `tRNS`, a transparent pixel's colour is its palette entry's or key's, not its own, so it imports as plain transparency and `provenance.hiddenRGB` says so. Neither representation claims to reconstruct the original drawing operations. Keep pose anchors/cues in their separately saved metadata; this import does not reconstruct them. Native Aseprite interchange is still future work.

## Aligned material passes

Scene assets may provide normal and emissive recipes, drawn by hand or generated (below). They must share color dimensions, frame names/order, durations, animations and atlas layout. Each exported asset directory has `color.png`, optional `normal.png`/`emissive.png`, and one common atlas JSON; with a normal pass it also has `normal_y_up.png` (green flipped to +Y up, as Godot and Unity read a normal map) and a Godot `canvas_texture.tres` that its `sprite_frames.tres` draws through ([game integration](game-integration.md#normal-and-emissive-passes)). `alignment.json` maps asset ids to those files. Each asset directory also has a Tiled `tileset.tsx`, and `scene.tmx` is the scene at its start as a Tiled map ([game integration](game-integration.md#the-scene-as-a-tiled-map)). Normal RGB encodes XYZ from −1 to +1: +X right, +Y down, +Z toward the viewer. Normals are renormalized for preview; their color channels are never palette-quantized.

`lighting:{ambient,bands,scope,lights:[{at,height,radius,color,strength,follow,flicker,keys}]}` supplies at most eight bounded preview lights, any of which may be a sun (below). Each light's diffuse brightness is banded deliberately into `bands` steps and then tinted by its color, so a colored light keeps one hue per ring; ambient light is not banded. Emission remains aligned to the current pose. With the default `scope: "passes"`, assets without passes keep their authored colors. `scope: "all"` lights them too, as flat surfaces facing the viewer, so a night scene darkens the terrain and props instead of only the assets with normal maps. Disabling lighting gives the ordinary color fallback. `mode: "ramp"` keeps the palette instead: each pixel's brightness (ambient plus banded lights; a light's colour then only sets its brightness) moves its colour along the ramps of its recipe, one step per `1 / steps` away from full brightness (`steps` 1–8, default 2), darker in shadow and up to `steps` lighter in a light's pool. Emissive pixels keep their colour, and colours on no ramp stay unlit. A torch-lit room then has exactly the colours of the unlit one. A placement with `lit: false` keeps its authored colours under any lighting: give it to fires, lamps, glowing magic and anything else that is a light source rather than something lit. Aligned passes are exported on a uniform grid even when the color recipe trims its own atlas.

**Lights that move.** A light can also have `strength` (0–8, default 1; 0 is off), `follow`, `flicker` and `keys`:

```json
"lights": [
  { "follow": { "instance": "torch-left", "point": "flame" }, "at": [0, -2], "height": 10, "radius": 56, "color": "#ffd38a", "flicker": { "amount": 0.3, "every": 90, "seed": 1 } },
  { "follow": { "instance": "hero", "point": "blade" }, "radius": 8, "strength": 0, "color": "#fff1c4",
    "keys": [{ "time": "strike+60" }, { "time": "strike+90", "strength": 2.5, "radius": 40, "ease": "out" }, { "time": "strike+300", "strength": 0, "radius": 8 }] }
]
```

- `follow` makes a light ride a uniquely named placement: where it stands, or with `point`, a named point of its current frame (a torch flame, a blade tip, a held lantern). `at` is then an offset (default none). The light moves with trajectories, animations and attachments, and is off while the placement is hidden or its frame has no such point.
- `flicker` swings the light's strength by up to `amount` (0–1) of it either way, and its reach by up to half as much, along seeded noise that changes about every `every` milliseconds (default 80). On average a flickering light is as bright as a steady one. It repeats once per scene `duration`, so a looping scene has no seam, and every light flickers on its own even with the same `seed`.
- `keys` set `strength` and `radius` over time, at milliseconds or cue times such as `"strike+90"`: held before the first key and after the last, eased from each key to the next by that key's `ease`. A key leaves out what it doesn't change; that keeps the light's own value. A flash is a short rise to a strength above 1 and a slower fall.
- In `mode: "ramp"`, strength above 1 lifts colours past their authored shade (up to `steps` lighter), which is what makes a flash read. In `multiply` mode a light never brightens a colour past its authored value.

**A sun.** `{"sun": [1, 0.5], "strength": 0.75, "color": "#fff4d8"}` is a light so far away that it falls the same way everywhere: no `at`, `height`, `radius` or `follow`, and no falloff, so a whole outdoor scene is lit alike. `sun` is how far a shadow falls across the ground per pixel of height, so it sets both where the sun stands and how high: `[1, 0.5]` is a low sun up and to the left whose shadows fall right and down, a little longer than their casters are tall; `[0.3, 0.15]` is the same sun near noon; `[0, 0]` is straight overhead and casts no shadows. Flat ground facing it gets `1 / sqrt(1 + x² + y²)` of its strength, banded like any light, and normal passes turn surfaces toward it or away. For daylight that keeps authored colours in `mode: "ramp"`, choose `ambient` and `strength` so lit ground comes to about 1: with `bands` 5, sun `[1, 0.5]` gives flat ground 0.75 of its strength, so `ambient` 0.45 and `strength` 0.75 keep sunlit colours and put shadows one step down. A sun takes `color`, `strength`, `flicker` and `keys` of strength (a cloud passing, a sunset). In the viewer it has no marker; choose it from the list, and its Shadow X and Y sliders set `sun`.

Renders report `lights`: where each light is, its radius and its strength at that time, or a sun's `sun` and strength. `pixel_scene` returns them too, and the viewer draws its markers there; dragging a following light moves its offset. This is a CPU material review, not a physically complete lighting model: shadows come only from placements with `shadow` ([cast shadows](#scene-and-tile-review)), and no fluid simulation, bloom, raymarching or material-mask pass is implemented. Measure preview cost separately from sprite-player performance before expanding it.

### Generated normal passes

A `pixelforge-normals` source ([normals.schema.json](../normals.schema.json)) derives a normal pass instead of drawing one: `{ "format": "pixelforge-normals", "version": 1, "recipe": "lamp.json" }` compiles with `pixelbolt compile lamp.normals.json --out lamp-normal.json`, or MCP `pixel_compile`, where the recipe may also be `{"revision": id}`. Each separate shape in a frame is rounded from its silhouette like a cushion: its rim leans outward and its thickest point faces the viewer, so props in one frame each get their own dome. `bevel` (1–64) rounds only that many pixels in from the edge and leaves a flat top. `detail` (0–1, default 0) raises lighter colours by up to 2 pixels to emboss brick, bark or rock; a colour on a ramp counts its step along that ramp, so two materials at the same step stay level, and any other colour counts its brightness. Transparent pixels are edges and the canvas border is not, so tiles and shapes cut by the frame stay open; frames with `wrap` read their slopes across the border and tile without seams.

The result is an ordinary recipe with the colour recipe's size, frame names and durations, animations and `sheet`. It covers exactly the colour silhouette, so its atlas has the same rectangles as the colour atlas, trimmed or not. Its palette names 17 directions: `5` faces the viewer, the digits around it lean 30° the way they sit around 5 on a numeric keypad (`8` up, `6` right, `1` down-left), and `q w e`, `a d`, `z x c` lean 60° the same way on a keyboard. A generated pass therefore reads as letters around the rims and digits across the faces, and a fix is written the same way: `a` on a left rim, `9` on a face turned up and right. [examples/forest-spirit.normals.json](../examples/forest-spirit.normals.json) compiles to [examples/forest-spirit-normal.json](../examples/forest-spirit-normal.json); `pixelbolt preview examples/forest-spirit-lit.scene.json` lights it, and you can move the light.

Inflating a silhouette cannot see forms its outline does not show. A tower or a box seen from the front comes out as a cushion, with ridges where its rims meet, and overlapping parts (an arm across the body) share one surface. Treat the pass as a start: light it from both sides in a scene, correct it with `patch`, and keep approved corrections in an overlay; they reapply to the frames a later compile leaves unchanged.

`faces` gives strong geometric forms, such as a tower, a wall or a crate, flat planes instead: it maps palette keys of the colour recipe to one direction each, and every pixel drawn in that key's colour takes it. A crate drawn with a top colour `t` and a front colour `f` takes `"faces": {"t": "w", "f": "5"}` in a side view; [depth and perspective](#depth-and-perspective) lists the keys for 3/4 and isometric views. Pixels in other colours, such as the outline, are rounded as before, and `detail` does not emboss faced pixels. The match is by the colour drawn, so a key that a frame `palette` recolours does not match in that frame. Metadata counts each faced key's pixels, and a key that draws no pixel is a warning.

Mirrored frames get mirrored normals, because the pass is derived from the drawn pixels. A hand-drawn normal recipe that mirrors with `flipX` (a frame, an operation or a mirrored pose) only moves its pixels: each keeps its left or right lean, so the mirrored frame lights from the wrong side. Derive those frames, or repaint their left and right keys. For engines that read normals with +Y up (Godot, Unity), see [game integration](game-integration.md#normal-and-emissive-passes).
