# Fluid physics model

This is the shared model behind `pixelforge-fluid`: the aim, units, scales, state and step order that every fluid feature builds on, so that features written separately (cohesion, bubbles and air pockets, sticky liquids, phase changes, plasma, and whatever comes next) still make sense together. It is a contract for contributors. User-facing behaviour is documented in [art-workflow.md](art-workflow.md).

To change something here, edit this file in its own small commit on `main` first, then build on it. Tell the other sessions working on fluids, since they build on it too.

## 0. The aim: results that look good and feel satisfying

The physics serves the picture. Every rule below exists because it makes motion read well in pixels and feel right to watch, not because it is accurate. When accuracy and looks disagree, looks win. Cheat in the open: name the cheat in a comment, keep it deterministic, keep it under an art control.

What "looks good and feels satisfying" means here:

- **Clear beats.** Motion has a readable shape: a build-up, the event (a splash, a snap, a strike, a pop), then a settle. Timing is set in frames and tuned by eye.
- **Things come to rest.** A finished motion settles into a still, clean shape. A pixel trembling for ever, a surface that never stops shimmering, or a heap that creeps without end is a bug, even if a solver would call it physical.
- **No noise without a meaning.** No lone pixels flickering, no ragged one-pixel bumps, no speckle. Every pixel that changes should be part of something the eye follows. Use dither and noise on purpose, never as leftovers.
- **Readable at 1×.** Shapes, silhouettes and bands read at the size they ship. Anything thinner than the grid can show is drawn on purpose (a strand, a bubble, a spark), not lost.
- **Exaggerate where it reads.** Bubbles can be bigger than real ones, steam can billow more, arcs can linger, and honey can hang longer. Push the effect to what reads best on the page.
- **Cause and effect across features.** A flame boils water, steam beads under a cold lid and drips back, ice is something bubbles slide under, and an arc that hits water makes it steam. This coherence is the reason for this document: a world whose parts agree is more satisfying to watch than any one accurate part.
- **Art controls, not physical constants.** Settings are named for what they look like (`rise`, `wobble`, `adhesion`, `expansion`) and have useful defaults. Ranges stop at what still looks good, not at what is physically possible.
- **Judged by eye.** Every feature ships with a preset and its GIF, reviewed frame by frame at 1× and 4×. Passing tests doesn't make it good.

## 1. Compatibility

- Every new behaviour is opt-in. A source that doesn't use a feature renders exactly as before, and the golden hashes in `test/goldens/fluid.json` do not move (`node scripts/goldens.mjs fluid` checks them). A shared refactor that changes existing pixels is a bug, unless it is a deliberate fix to how something looks, regenerated and described in its commit.
- Determinism as in `AGENTS.md`: only + − × ÷ and sqrt, `craft.js` trigonometry and seeded hashing, fixed substeps and iteration counts.

## 2. Units

| Quantity | Unit |
|---|---|
| Length | pixel (one grid cell) |
| Time | frame; a step is `dt = 1 / substeps` of a frame |
| Velocity, acceleration | pixels per frame, pixels per frame² (`MAX_SPEED` caps velocity) |
| Amount of matter | cells' worth of liquid: one full cell is 1 (`PER_CELL` = 4 particles, so a particle is ¼). Ice and vapour use the same unit, so matter can be counted across phases |
| Pressure | pixels of liquid head: the hydrostatic pressure `h` pixels down a still pool is `h` (gravity × depth, in the solver's scaling). Air-pocket stiffness and similar settings are given in head, so "a pocket under 10 pixels of water shrinks by a third" can be read off directly |
| Temperature | the heat scale below |
| Voltage | volts, an art scale: `breakdown` is volts per pixel of gap (see section 10) |

## 3. Temperature: one heat scale for every phase

Gas `temperature` already defines it: 0 is ambient (room air), and the fire presets draw dull red at about 0.3 and a white core at 1 (campfire and torch emitters run at 1.1, the steam vent at 0.8). Liquids, ice, vapour and plasma use the same scale, with no second one. Default thresholds for water, as proposals the phase-change prototype may tune; the order must hold:

| Event | Default | Note |
|---|---|---|
| freeze | −0.5 | ice melts at the same −0.5, but only once it has taken up its latent heat (and water freezes only once it has given it up), so the latent plateau is the gap that stops flicker; no separate melt threshold |
| ambient | 0 | `ambient` per effect (default 0) is what everything relaxes toward |
| boil | 0.5 | below the flame range, so a flame boils water and the steam vent's 0.8 is steam |
| dew (condense) | 0.4 | vapour touching something below it condenses; defaults to `boil` − 0.1 |
| flame | 0.3 to 1 | existing fire looks |
| ionize (thermal) | 2 | above every flame preset, so fire never turns into plasma by accident; arcs reach 2 to 4 |

Temperature is a temperature, not an energy: every phase has the same heat capacity per unit of matter, and exchange rates are art controls. Latent heat is a per-particle progress toward the change, filled while the temperature is held at the threshold, so melting and boiling take visible time (a plateau, not a snap). Sublimation and deposition are switches per material, not a pressure-dependent phase diagram.

## 4. Matter and the ledger

- States: **solid** (obstacles, and frozen particles, which are ice), **liquid** (particles), **gas** (grid fields: air, smoke, vapour) and **plasma** (gas with ionization above 0).
- One liquid material per liquid effect: its own settings (viscosity, yield, tension, adhesion, thresholds). Its ice and its vapour are the same material in other states. Mixing several liquids in one effect is out of scope for now.
- Phase changes conserve matter exactly: a particle that freezes is ice worth ¼; one that evaporates adds ¼ to the vapour field's matter. How much room vapour takes up on screen is `expansion`, a look applied when vapour is drawn and pushes the air, not a change in the matter count.
- The metadata keeps a per-frame ledger of liquid, ice and vapour matter, plus what emitters, drains and open edges added or took away. A closed box keeps the sum constant; tests check that.

## 5. The grid and its layers

One grid per effect, one cell per pixel. Every feature reads and writes these shared layers rather than keeping a private copy:

- **Solid layer**: which cells are solid, who owns each one (an obstacle index, `ICE`, or a closed edge beyond the canvas), each solid cell's velocity (a moving obstacle's, from its keys; a piece of ice's own while it settles or floats, zero once it rests), and each solid's surface properties (section 6).
- **Liquid**: particles with position, velocity, the APIC slopes, and, when heat is on, temperature and phase progress. `fluid` (cells holding particles) is the pressure solve's active set; `wet` (density ≥ `LEVEL`) is what looks and sparks treat as water.
- **Gas**: face velocities, `smoke` density, `vapour` matter, `temperature`, `ionization`. In a gas effect it covers every open cell. In a liquid effect it covers the air cells (section 7) and costs nothing unless a feature needs it.
- **Electric**: potential, conductivity and current (section 10), only when something has a voltage.

## 6. Solids

One function builds the solid layer each step; every feature goes through it. Its parts:

- Static obstacles, keyed moving obstacles (velocity from their keys, measured from where they really are, not from the whole pixel they are drawn at), and ice cells.
- Surface properties are fields on the obstacle source and apply wherever they make sense:
  - `adhesion` (sticky liquids): how strongly liquid holds on.
  - `surface`: pixels or smooth.
  - `temperature` (phase changes): a hot plate or a cold window. In a gas effect it heats or cools the air.
  - `voltage` and `conductor` (plasma).
- Ice takes its surface properties from its liquid: water sticks to its own ice with the effect's adhesion, and ice conducts electricity poorly. Ice that settles or floats is a moving solid like a keyed obstacle, so what clings to it moves with it and bubbles slide under it as it drifts.
- Whatever a feature does at a solid it does at every kind of solid. Bubbles slide under ice as under a ledge, sticky liquid clings to ice, air pockets are bounded by ice, and arcs treat ice as an insulator. Never special-case "obstacle" when the rule is about "solid".

## 7. Air

- In a liquid effect, every cell that is neither solid nor `fluid` is air. Air joined to an open edge is at pressure 0. A sealed region of air is an air pocket: when pockets are on, it keeps its amount of air and its pressure follows the gas law, in pixels of head.
- A bubble is a pocket too small for the grid to show, carried as a spark. It holds an amount of air (its `vol`) and rises against gravity. When it reaches open air it releases that air, and any vapour in it, to the air. A pocket that shrinks below a few cells becomes bubbles, and bubbles that gather under a ceiling can merge into a pocket.
- Vapour and smoke in a liquid effect ride a gas velocity field over the air cells. Liquid and ice act on it as walls moving with the liquid. Vapour that boils into a sealed pocket adds to it and raises its pressure.
- The gas over air cells is the same gas code that `kind: "gas"` runs, not a second copy. Long term, gas and liquid effects are one simulation: `kind` only chooses what emitters and fills add by default.
- As built (`src/fluid.js`): bubbles are sparks with `rise`. Any stage makes one with `addBubble(run.sparks, run.totals, s, x, y, r, extra)` (`s` as `readSparks` reads it, `extra` its own fields, such as a boil's vapour), and every bubble that ends goes to `moveSparks`'s `ended` hook with `end` set to `'air'`, `'life'`, `'solid'` or `'edge'`. A solid that moves onto a bubble pushes it aside. Bubbles' `lift` reaches the force stage through `state.lifts`. With `air` on, `sealAir` labels the air each step (`state.air.label`, one entry in `state.air.regions` per sealed or open region) and tracks each pocket's `air`, the volume it would fill at zero pressure; a pocket is one more unknown in the liquid's pressure solve. `addAir(state, i, j, amount)` adds air or vapour to the pocket at a cell (a bubble that ends there does it). Air reads the solver's solid masks (`g.solid`, `g.pixels`, `uFixed`, `vFixed`), so ice must reach those to bound pockets and stop bubbles. Sealed gaps of 3 to 11 cells become bubbles, not pockets. Bubbles waiting under a ceiling that touch but cannot merge (a bubble holds at most 12 square pixels and must fit where it waits, so under a flat ceiling bubbles stop growing at six) and hold `GATHER` (8) square pixels of air between them become a pocket: `carvePocket` clears that many cells under the ceiling (at least 12, the highest first, then the nearest along it), moves the liquid in them down its column to the first cell that stays liquid (the pressure spreads it from there, so the level rises elsewhere), and `air.made` hands their air to the region those cells are in at the next `sealAir`: a new pocket starts with it, a pocket it joins adds it. A boil's bubbles, which carry vapour, stay bubbles. A pocket whose region reaches open air vents: its air joins the open air at pressure 0. In a loop, bubbles that push the water (with `lift`, or any with `air` on) are made from the warm-up on and drawn only if born in the recorded cycle (`born`, as a boil's are), and the loop simulates on until the `air.bubbles` alive at its end have ended.
- A body of liquid sealed in on every side by solids and ice, with no air, pocket or open edge beside it, cannot change its volume: the pressure solve (`sealedBodies`) gives up the sum of what such a body asks for (crowded cells spreading, a wall moving in), shared evenly over it. Asked for more, the solve ran off to huge pressures.

## 8. Forces and up

- Each effect has one gravity vector. "Up" is against it, everywhere: hot gas, vapour and plasma rise along it (buoyancy from temperature), smoke and dry-ice fog sink with `weight`, and bubbles rise against it relative to the water. A gas effect has no gravity setting, so its up stays −y.
- Cohesion (`tension`) acts between liquid and liquid, and adhesion between liquid and solid. Both are measured relative to the solid's own motion, so what clings to a lifted spoon rises with it.
- A bubble's `lift` hands part of its buoyancy to the water around it. Boiling uses the same path, so a boil churns the water.

## 9. Heat flow and phase changes

- Heat is carried by whatever holds it: particles, ice cells, and the gas temperature field. Heat flows by conduction (between neighbours and across contact: particle to air, particle to solid, air to solid), relaxes toward `ambient` at a rate (gas `cooling` is this rate), and comes from sources: emitter and fill temperatures, obstacle `temperature`, and Joule heating from current.
- Freezing and melting turn particles into ice and back. A cell holding enough ice becomes solid in the solid layer. Ice needs a seed: water below freeze turns to ice only where it touches ice or a solid colder than freeze, or once it is well below (0.5), so a pond chilled from above freezes in from its cold shore instead of all at once. Ice that touches no solid and has nothing under it settles as one piece; water flanking most of it holds it up (it floats).
- Evaporation and condensation move ¼ units between particles and the vapour field. Vapour touching a cold solid or ice gathers there as dew, drawn as water, and drips off as a drop once a cell holds a cell's worth; onto cold water it joins the water a particle at a time. Open cold vapour does not condense (there is no mist state), for three reasons. Turned into water particles, which are a quarter of a cell and fall, it makes every steam plume rain. What mist looks like on screen is already there: `vapour.look` draws vapour as steam or fog, and vapour cooled below the room's air stops rising and sinks (buoyancy is relative to `ambient`), as cold mist does, so the dry-ice fog and a cooling plume read as mist. And mist that is not rain would be a third state, liquid drifting with the air, with its own field, ledger entry, look and rules for settling, evaporating and joining dew, which no preset needs yet. Cold surfaces still take the vapour as dew. Boiling inside the liquid makes bubbles (vapour bubbles, section 7) before vapour leaves the surface: a boil should look like a boil.
- Sublimation (ice to vapour) and deposition (vapour to ice) are per-material switches. Deposition grows frost as fern-like fingers, only from cold solids and the frost already there and only into air below freezing: a finger starts at seeded sites on a cold surface (most in its corners, where the cold is on two sides), grows straight ahead of its tip and forks at 45° by a seeded draw into shorter branches a pixel apart, and stops short of other fingers. Judged at 1× and 4×: a rime grown off every cold cell read as a ragged rim, fixed sites with branches as long as their stems as a maze, and branches off neighbouring cells as solid bands. A sublimating material never makes liquid: its vapour comes back only as frost.
- As built (`src/fluid.js`): `state.heat` holds the heat field of a liquid with `heat`. Each particle keeps one `energy` (its temperature plus its latent progress; `tempOf` and `energyOf` convert) and a `frozen` flag; a cell with two ice particles (`ICE_CELL`) is ice. `heatStep` runs at the start of every step, before the liquid (stages 4 and 5): heat flow, freezing and melting (seeded by touching ice or a solid below freeze, or `SUPERCOOL` 0.5 below it), boiling and evaporation, condensation and deposition, and on a frame's first step `settleIce`. Frost cells keep `frostDir`, `frostGen` (stem, branch, twig) and `frostLen` (cells along their finger); a branch starts only off an odd `frostLen`, `heat.branch` (default 0.5) is how often, and a start site is `SITES` (0.04) of the cells beside a flat cold side and nine times that in a corner, with a slow fallback (`NUCLEI`, 0.003 a frame) on any stretch with no frost within `SPACING` (5) pixels, so every cold surface frosts in time. A frost cell that loses its ice forgets its finger (`forgetFrost`; `frostIce` notes the cells that have held frost), a cell that has started taking frost goes on only beside frost or a cold solid, and `settleIce` joins frost to its piece across corners, so a finger grown at 45 degrees holds together under gravity (taken four ways, its branches fell and grew again in mid-air). Then `heatMasks` writes ice into the solver's masks (`g.solid`, `g.pixels`, `uFixed`, `vFixed`, so pockets and bubbles see it) and the solid layer (owner `ICE`, a settling piece's velocity through `solidLayer`'s ice hook), and refreshes what clings. Obstacle temperatures are read through the layer's `props(owner).temperature` (`surfaceTemperatures`). Vapour (`heat.vapour`) is matter in particles' worth on a grid of its own whose walls are the solids and the cells holding two or more particles (moving with the liquid), stepped by `stepGas` with an `over` hook for its forces (along up, relative to `ambient`), its sources and its carrying, which rescales semi-Lagrangian advection to keep the matter. Anything that adds vapour goes through `ventVapour`, which calls `addAir` when the cell is in a sealed pocket; a boil bubble is `addBubble(…, { steam, born })` and its end goes through `endedOf` to `ventBubble`. A loop draws only the boil bubbles born in its recorded cycle, wrapped round to frame 0 as sparks are, and simulates on (up to a cycle) until they have ended; those born in the warm-up still carry vapour and lift the water, unseen. (Drawn as they came, the warm-up's bubbles stood at frame 0 where the cycle's own should have been, and bubbles popped in and out at the seam.) The ledger is `heatAmounts`: `water`, `ice`, `vapour`, `added`, `taken`. Heat works with keyed and smooth obstacles: `state.heat` keeps the obstacles' current `layout`, pixels (`base`) and `places`, which `moveObstacles` sets before it calls `heatMasks`, so the solid layer always holds the obstacles where they are, with their velocities, and the ice; `mask` is the obstacles' pixels and the ice's, and beside smooth obstacles the geometry is traced again from it whenever it changes (ice meets the liquid pixel by pixel), and dropped once no smooth obstacle shows. Vapour faces between a moving obstacle and the air carry its velocity, and a particle in a smooth obstacle's open corner touches that obstacle (its heat, and freezing's seed).

## 10. Electricity and plasma

- Obstacles with `voltage` are electrodes; `conductor: true` marks metal. Closed edges may be grounded.
- The potential is solved over conductivity: very low in neutral air (it conducts only once broken down), high in plasma (rising with ionization), moderate in liquid water, low in ice, and high in conductors.
- Air ionizes when the field exceeds `breakdown` (lower in hot or thin gas, so arcs follow hot air), or thermally above the ionize threshold. Channels grow by seeded dielectric breakdown.
- Current heats the gas, and any water or ice it passes through, on the shared heat scale. Plasma recombines, faster as it cools, leaving an afterglow and a leftover channel that the next strike follows.
- Because water conducts and heat is shared, an arc that reaches a pool goes to it and makes it steam with no special case.
- As built (`src/electric.js`): obstacles read `ELECTRODE_FIELDS` (`voltage`, a number or keys, `resistance`, `charge`, `conductor`); a decoration with a voltage is an electrode the gas flows through. Every solid in `state.solids` that is not an electrode or metal insulates, so ice will insulate with no change. The electric step opens each gas step: conductivity (plasma 8 × ionization², neutral air 8 / 100000, metal 64), the potential by conjugate gradients, the supplies' circuit, Joule heat (`electric.heating` × current²) into the shared `temperature`, then breakdown growth. Plasma is a graph of markers riding the gas velocity; `ionization` and `current` are look fields. Thermal plasma (`ionize`, default 2, 0 for none) starts at the threshold and is full at 1.5 times it, in gas only: never inside an insulator, whose cells the vapour's temperature holds at the obstacle's own (a hot plain obstacle read as plasma grew arcs as if it were grounded metal). Conductivities for every state are `CONDUCTIVITY` there: air 0.00008, plasma 8 × ionization², water 0.5, ice 0.0008, metal 64.
- Over a liquid (one with `heat` and `vapour`; `electric` or an electrode without both is refused): the gas is the vapour's gas over the air cells, whose flow carries the plasma and whose `temperature` the current heats, so a hot arc rises with the vapour's `buoyancy`. The field is solved over the whole grid: obstacles insulate unless they are electrodes or metal, and each step the liquid hands the layer its `matter` (`MATTER.water` for cells holding `ICE_CELL` particles or more, `MATTER.ice` where that is mostly ice), which conducts at `CONDUCTIVITY.water` or `.ice`. Water takes an arc that reaches it as a grounded edge does: an arc's foot that lands on it stays where it struck (dragged along the surface by the steam it raises, it drew long channels over the water that the next stroke ran down) and charges through it, but the water grows no streamers of its own (an approaching leader would otherwise sprout them off every wave). Plasma never enters water or ice: a marker the liquid covers goes out. Joule heat is current² ÷ conductivity, so water and ice cells take `heating` × current² × (plasma's 8 ÷ their conductivity) a frame, water 16 times a full channel's for the same current: the current spreads out as soon as it enters a pool, and the water at the arc's foot is where it is dense enough to boil. That heat goes into `state.heat.input`, which `heatStep` adds to every particle in the cell, so the steam leaves through the ordinary vapour path; the electric step runs before heat (stage 3). The vapour's look draws `ionization` and `current`. Water makes the field slower to solve, so `electric.iterations` defaults to 48 over a liquid (24 in a gas); and since a network driven by its supplies holds every potential between theirs, a supplied electrode's potential is held to that range (an unconverged solve after a restrike drove a −180 V supply to −390 V and boiled a quarter of the pond).
- The voltage scale: `breakdown` is volts per pixel of gap, 3 by default, so a gap of g pixels strikes from about 3g volts (less across hot air, and much less from a sharp tip, where the field gathers). Once an arc joins two electrodes its current is about voltage ÷ (resistance + arc length ÷ 8); it stays lit from `sustain` (0.5) and goes out below half of it. The presets: a Jacob's ladder at 30 V with resistance 20 across a 3-pixel gap at breakdown 4; lightning at −240 V with resistance 20 across 80 pixels at breakdown 2.5; a plasma globe pulsed at 120 V with resistance 3. Joule heat `electric.heating` × current² a frame brings an arc's air to 2 to 4 on the heat scale. Lightning into a pond: −180 V with resistance 20 across about 44 pixels at breakdown 2.5 and heating 0.05, the pond grounded through an electrode at 0 V lining its bowl, boils six to ten cells' worth of water a stroke.

## 10a. Grains: sand and other granular matter

- A liquid effect's material may be granular (`granular`): sand, salt, sugar, gravel, dry snow. It is the liquid's own particles and grid, with friction in place of viscosity and elasticity, so it shares the solid layer, the ledger's unit (a grain particle is ¼ of a cell, like a drop) and the step order. It has no cohesion unless asked (`tension` defaults to 0) and never springs back.
- What makes it read as sand: it holds a slope up to its angle of repose at any height (a yield stress holds a heap only up to a height), it comes to a dead stop, and it moves by avalanches down its surface rather than by spreading. The art control is the angle itself, `repose` in degrees; `cohesion` (damp sand) lets it stand steeper and hold small overhangs.
- Friction depends on pressure (Drucker–Prager, after Zhu and Bridson, "Animating sand as a fluid", 2005, simplified). After the pressure solve, a wet cell whose rate of shear this step is at most sin(`repose`) × its pressure (the liquid's own, in the solver's units, plus `cohesion`) holds: the friction there can stop the deformation within the step. Cells that hold and touch form pieces that move as rigid bodies (each piece's velocity becomes its mean motion and spin), and a piece that presses on a solid moves with that solid (static friction), so a heap rests exactly. Elsewhere the grains slide, resisted by a friction stress of that size: an implicit viscosity of sin(`repose`) × pressure ÷ the rate of shear, capped.
- Particles in a holding piece take the grid's velocity (no FLIP share), so a resting heap has no jitter.
- Grains do not mix with the liquid-only features: no `yield` (elastic), no `heat` (sand neither freezes nor boils on the water scale), no `air` (air passes through sand) and so no electricity over it. They meet every solid, keyed and smooth included, so a plough pushes a heap and grains slide down a smooth slope until it is shallower than their repose.
- As built (`src/fluid.js`, `grainFriction`, `grainPieces`): grains split their pressure solve as the yield path does (the flow's, then the spacing's), and read friction from the flow's pressure. The friction is tan(`repose`) ÷ 1.07, so a settled bed whose gravity is tilted holds its repose to within two degrees from 15 to 45 (past about 47 the coefficient passes 1 and a dry column no longer falls, so `repose` stops at 45). Landing: the fall the pressure solve stopped in a cell, up to its weight (dt × gravity), costs the motion across gravity friction × that (Coulomb), and anything above the weight is lost outright, and the push across gravity the pressure solve gives a face is held to one weight a step, so a stream lands dead and doesn't dig a crater. Only cells pressed by at least a quarter of a cell's weight hold, so a falling stream never joins the heap it lands on. Pieces keep the faces inside them and those open to air; a face shared with sliding grains stays as solved. Held cells mend their spacing only inside the heap (surface cells keep theirs), and a grain slower than 0.1 pixels a frame lying on held grains or a solid sleeps, so a heap comes to rest exactly.

## 11. Step order

Each step runs these stages in order. A feature fills in its stage and costs nothing when it is off:

1. **Solids**: place obstacles and their velocities, and add ice. Build the solid layer.
2. **Sources**: emitters, fills on their frame, heat sources, electrodes.
3. **Electric**: potential, ionization growth and decay, current, Joule heat.
4. **Heat**: conduction and relaxation across every phase, and latent progress.
5. **Phase changes**: freeze and melt, evaporate and condense, sublimate and deposit. Update the ledger.
6. **Liquid**: particles to grid, forces (gravity, cohesion, bubble lift), viscosity (walls moving at the solid's velocity), pressure (conjugate gradients, air pockets at their gas-law pressure), for grains friction from that pressure and their rigid pieces, grid to particles, adhesion relative to solids, advection that never enters a solid.
7. **Gas**: forces (buoyancy along up, weight), advection, pressure over its cells (liquid and solids as moving walls), then carry smoke, vapour, temperature and ionization.
8. **Particles drawn over the fluid**: sparks, bubbles, whitewater, carriers.
9. **Bookkeeping**: ledger, figures, warnings.

## 13. Rigid bodies

`pixelforge-bodies` (`src/bodies.js`) is a separate compiler for balls, boxes, polygons and hoops, not a fluid stage: it keeps none of the fluid's state and the fluid keeps none of its. It follows section 0 (things come to rest: slow bodies sleep and hold exactly still; cheats such as `tireless` are named art controls) and section 1's determinism, and uses section 2's units: pixels, frames, pixels a frame and a frame², with gravity as one vector per effect. Pixel obstacles there are the same symbols, solid wherever a character is not a dot or space.

Bodies meet each other by sequential impulses in substeps. A free body resting on top of one more than ten times lighter (the contact facing within 60 degrees of gravity, and no impact) is solved as if it weighed ten times the lighter: sequential impulses settle a load as slowly as the weights under it differ, and a plank fifty times as dense as the balls under it sank into them, bounced out and hopped for ever, while a heavy box on a light one slid off it. A hit or a shove keeps the true weights, so a heavy body still ploughs through light ones and keeps its speed. Each body is drawn as a picture that may reach past its shape (a ball of radius 1.5 is a 3 × 3 square), so touching bodies' pictures can claim the same pixel; it is drawn for the body whose shape covers the pixel's middle more deeply, never simply the one drawn last, so touching bodies are drawn side by side, each in its own pixels (a cutter, which passes through what it cuts, is drawn over it).

Bodies meet a fluid (water, a thick liquid, sand, or a gas) as `bodies` in an effect of `pixelforge-fluid`, the same entries a bodies source takes. The fluid owns the grid; the bodies keep their own solver, and the two trade once a frame, and a free body in a liquid within each of the liquid's pressure solves too:

- **Bodies push the liquid.** Each body is a moving solid in the solid layer (section 6): the pixels it draws are solid cells, owned by it, moving at its velocity. Each of its cells moves at the body's velocity plus its turning (v + ω × r), so the pressure solve treats its faces as walls moving with it, a spinning paddle stirs the water and a wheel turns a stream. It shoves the liquid in the cells it newly covers out along its move there (or straight out from its centre, when that cell hardly moves), so a crate dropped into a pool throws a splash and one dropped onto sand throws a crater; a free body in a liquid pays for the speed it gives the liquid it shoves (they meet as one knock that sticks), so a stone throws what it lands on nearly at its own speed and a light plank landing flat is stopped by it. A cell it leaves fills at once from the liquid in the open cells beside it, so a turning paddle leaves no hole of air behind it under water. (The liquid in a cell it has just covered is moving, not beside it: counted, it went from under a plank sinking into a pool's top row onto the top of it, a sheet of water that held it under.) Sand isn't turned by a body's turning (its faces move with the body's velocity alone): a crate tumbling on sand would only dig itself in.
- **The liquid's pressure moves the free bodies.** A body that moves freely (not `fixed`, not asleep) in a liquid (not sand) is three more unknowns of the liquid's pressure solve, its velocity and its turning, so the liquid and the body share each push in one solve (Batty, Bertails and Bridson, 2007): the body's faces move as it yields, through the frame's steps, and what the solves did to it joins its velocity before its own step. Each step the solve first finds the pressure with the body's faces moving as the liquid beside them does (the pressure that holds the liquid up, the churn of a surface's sparse top cells, a stream along the body), which the body doesn't yield to, and then the pressure its motion through that liquid makes, which it does. Moved by its velocity alone, a body was a piston of endless mass: a light plank dropped flat drove the water out from under it as fast as it fell and threw a third of a pool over the walls. Now the water's whole inertia meets it, and it stops on the water in a frame. (Left out instead, the pressure solved for the liquid with the body held still stopped a body sinking through water every step; a still pool's head left a crate floating high, by the churn's extra head; a mean of each cell's pressure over time lagged the push of the liquid a bobbing body sets moving, and fed it.)
- **The liquid pushes the bodies.** After the frame's liquid steps, each body counts its **submerged** cells: the rows of it (across gravity) with liquid beside them. Buoyancy is the weight of that much liquid, against gravity, at the submerged cells' centre, so a body of `density` 0.5 floats half out and a boat rights itself (a square half as dense as water floats on its corner, as a real one does). The level it floats at is read once a frame, in the columns beside it at the row of its lowest point (a plank whose cells are all over the water can dip into it), counting the sparse cells over the top wet one by what they hold, and up to three columns further out where the column beside it is dry; read from the wet cells alone, the level stood half a pixel low and a plank a pixel thick floated a row too deep, in the pool's full top row. It is held for the frame; the bodies take the area of the body's exact shape under it, and that part's centre, afresh at every substep, so the lift follows the body as it bobs and rolls within the frame (held as one force for the frame, the lift was a spring stepped a frame at a time, which gained energy, and a light plank rocked on and tipped). Within three cells of a body the liquid's particles take the grid's velocity (no FLIP share): the noise FLIP keeps at a moving body's faces otherwise fed a floating body every frame. Liquid density is 1, the matter unit of section 4. Every face of the body against liquid **drags** it toward the velocity of the liquid a cell further out (the cell it touches moves with it, and dragged toward its own wake a body pumps itself up and down), solved implicitly so any drag is stable: water's drag grows with the speed between them, as real drag does, and a thick liquid's with its viscosity. A free body the pressure solve moves is dragged along each face only: the push across it is the solve's (dragged across its faces too, it met the water twice, and a light crate righting itself crept so slowly that it fell asleep tilted). An added mass of half its submerged liquid joins its mass for the change, so a light ball doesn't shudder.
- **What rests on a body weighs on it.** Above each upper face, the liquid or sand in the column over it, up to where it ends, presses down there with its weight, at that face, so sand poured on one end of a plank tips it. Liquid below the level of the liquid around the body is left out: its weight is already in the body's buoyancy.
- **Sand holds bodies up as ground does.** Grains don't float a body. Each frame the bodies meet the sand that is at rest as they meet an obstacle, so a crate lands on a heap, digs in a pixel or two where its landing stirs the sand, and stands; sand that moves drags them hard toward its own motion, so a crate on a slope that avalanches rides down with it. This ground is soft: it stops a body sinking into it but never throws one out, so sand that flows in under a resting body holds it up where it is instead of tipping it on end.
- **In a gas** a body pushes the gas as a solid; the gas's pressure on each face pushes it back, and the air drags it toward its flow, a little, growing with the speed between them, so a blast throws crates; nothing floats in air. Much of that pressure is the body's own motion met by the air, so, as in a liquid, half the air it fills joins its mass against the pushes (the solve takes air, as water, to weigh a unit a cell), but not against its weight: met a frame at a time without it, a light crate overshot every frame, back and forth at the speed limit. The effect's `gravity` pulls the bodies (a gas has none of its own: hot gas rises along up, section 8).
- **With heat** a body may have a `temperature`, as an obstacle does, and keeps it, so a red-hot stone boils the water it lands in. Ice is ground to bodies, as resting sand is.
- **One step order.** Each frame: solids first (section 11, stage 1), with the bodies placed where their last step left them; the liquid's stages; then the bodies, what the liquid's pressure solves did to them and its buoyancy and drag applied to their velocities, step through their own substeps against each other, the walls and the obstacles. They are drawn where the liquid met them.
- **Rest.** A body in still liquid sleeps as one in air does, so a floating crate comes to rest exactly; liquid that moves it more than its wake speed wakes it, and so does an obstacle that moves within a pixel of it; a moving obstacle's faces move at its velocity, so a crate rides a shelf as it moves. A body is drawn at a whole pixel and steps to the next as it passes halfway there the way it last moved, but steps back only once it is clearly past halfway (and its angle likewise), so one that rocks about a boundary on the water holds still instead of flickering while one that drifts steps evenly; drawn finer than the liquid (`subpixels`), it holds its fine pixel and its angle the same way. Its solid cells are where it really is (the pixels whose centres its exact shape covers); a free body in a liquid holds them, taking a cell once its shape covers the cell's centre by a quarter of a pixel and giving it back once the centre is a quarter of a pixel outside, so a light crate bobbing about a row doesn't shove a row of the liquid out and take it back every frame, which kept it bobbing. The liquid is drawn in those of them under its surface wherever the body's drawing leaves them uncovered, so no hole opens under a floating ball as it bobs, and a surface the bodies stir is drawn without the dents a pixel deep and up to three across that its ripples cut into the grid (sand and a boil keep theirs). Drawing never moves a body or the liquid.
- **Loops.** In a looping effect the bodies run through the warm-up with the fluid, and the closing `blend` frames draw them blended toward where they were in the warm-up's closing frames, as the fields are, so the last frame flows into the first; on the last, a free body in a liquid stands in the cells it held there. A fixed body that turns other than a whole number of its own symmetric turns a cycle (a box's half turn, a hoop's side) is warned about.
- **The live runner** steps the bodies a frame at a time with the fluid; a push moves the bodies it reaches as well as the fluid.
- **Not yet:** a body neither warms nor cools itself, nothing floats in a gas, and fluid inside a hollow body (a hoop holding water) doesn't weigh on it.

### 13a. Teams, painted ground and power-ups

A bodies effect may lay a **territory** over its canvas: a grid of square cells, each owned by one team (or by none), drawn in its team's colour. It is ground in the solid layer's sense (section 6), but whose ground it is depends on who meets it:

- **Teams.** A body with a `team` passes through cells its team owns and meets every other team's cells as a wall, with the cells' bounce and friction; a body without a team meets every owned cell. Bodies meet each other as they always do, teams or not. Pixel obstacles and closed edges stay walls to everyone.
- **Painting.** A cell a team's body really hits (the solver had to push it off the cell, as a hit counts in section 13) becomes the body's team's at the end of that substep, and the walls are traced again before the next one. The cell is the one behind the face it hit, so a ball that strikes a corner takes the corner cell. A body of the cell's own team inside it holds it: painted under that body, it would leave the body inside another team's ground, walls on every side. A count of each team's cells is kept every frame: the score, which the metadata reports and a heads-up text can show.
- **Held speed.** A body with `speed` is set back to that speed, along its own direction of travel, at the end of every substep: an open art cheat, as `tireless` is, so a ball crossing a board in zero gravity never tires and never runs away. A body that has stopped dead keeps no direction and is left alone. It bounces off whatever it meets at any approach, however glancing (a slower approach than an impact, which lets a dropped ball settle, left it sliding along the wall for good).
- **Power-ups.** A pickup is a square at a place, there from one frame to another, taken by the first body (in body order) whose shape reaches it, and a scripted event is the same power given to a named body at a frame. A power acts at the end of a frame, between the bodies' steps, and only through the body's own state: `split` makes copies of the taker turned evenly about its heading, `grow` scales a ball's radius (its mass and inertia follow its area) for some frames, `speed` scales its held speed for some frames (to at most the speed limit), `warp` moves it to a place and, for a team's body, makes the cells it lands on its team's (the pocket it carves from), `magnet` pulls bodies of the other teams toward a point with a steady force (as gravity does, through the substeps) for some frames, `vanish` takes the body away (an event may take a fixed one: a ring bursts), waking what rested on it, and `break` breaks it into pieces (below). A power that moves or reshapes a sleeping body wakes it and what rests on it (`split`, `grow` and its end, `warp`), and a magnet wakes the sleeping bodies it pulls as it starts and as it stops. A `tireless` body keeps what a power gives it (a speed, the height it is warped to, a cut's push) on top of what it had. A strike is a power given to every body a named body pushed in the frame (a hit or a push along: a contact the solver pushed), each body at most once every `rest` frames: a blade whose strike is `split` multiplies whatever it touches. A strike with a `cut` makes its body a cutter while the strike works: its contacts with free bodies are left out of the solve, so it passes through them, and a contact that touches (or closes to a touch within the substep) strikes the body, with the way behind the cutter there, against its face's motion past the body. What a cut strikes leaves that way at the cut's speed on top of its own velocity (a split's pieces fanned about it), so the pieces lie in the blade's wake; pushed instead, they were swept along ahead of the blade and struck again and again. A split taker whose fills step with time takes the frame's fill with its copies: each split is a new generation. Copies are made where the taker is and part along their headings (coincident circles push apart along the way they move apart), and splits stop at the effect's `most` bodies. Nothing is random: the taker, the copies' headings and the order of powers on one frame are fixed by the source (strikes go in body order).
- **Breaking.** A body that `breaks` is knocked, at a point where something meets it, by the speed they meet at (with their bounce), shared out by how easily each side gives there (its inverse mass plus its inverse inertia times the square of its arm about the contact's normal): a body hitting a wall takes the whole of it, a pebble hitting a heavy vase almost none. The knock is read from the speed before the solve, not from the impulses the solve passes along a chain of touching bodies, which broke every crate in a row at once. At the end of a substep, each body knocked at or past its `at` breaks: seeded sites in its shape, drawn toward the point it was hit, cut it into the cells of their Voronoi diagram (each cell its outline cut by the bisectors with the other sites, so convex), and each pixel of its picture goes with the cell of the nearest site; a site whose cell would hold fewer pixels than the picture's share (at least 4) is dropped and the cut made again. Each piece is a convex polygon body placed on the half pixel nearest its centroid (the middle of its padded picture), with the body's density, its area and moment, the velocity its part had (velocity plus the turning about the body's centre) and a kick of `speed` away from the point hit; the body is gone, what rested on it wakes, and pieces never break again. The `break` power does the same at the end of a frame, about a pickup's place or the body's centre.
- **Pivots.** A fixed body turns about its centre, or about a pivot of its own (a blade about its hilt): the pivot moves at the body's velocity, the body's centre goes round it, and the body's faces move at the pivot's velocity plus the turn about it, so what the blade sweeps is pushed as hard at its tip as a free body would be.
- **Long matches.** A match may run past a recipe's 256 frames when it is written as numbered video frames: the same world stepped frame after frame, each frame drawn and kept as an image, never a recipe.

## 14. Joints, ropes and cloth

`pixelforge-bodies` also carries articulated constraints and cloth. They are optional fields of the same effect and use the same units, gravity, obstacles, walls, body records and one-shot compile. An effect may have bodies, cloths or both; `bodies` may be empty when `cloths` or `ropes` are present.

### 14a. Joints

A joint connects two named bodies, or a body and a fixed world point. Anchors are local to their body (`anchorA`, `anchorB`); a world end's anchor is the world point. Types:

- **distance**: both ends stay `length` apart. The solver corrects both stretching and compression.
- **rope**: only pulls. It is free while the distance is at most `max`, and becomes a distance constraint past it.
- **spring**: a soft distance constraint with `stiffness` and `damping`.
- **hinge**: the two anchors stay together; the bodies turn freely around them, optionally within a relative `limit` in degrees.
- **weld**: the anchors and the relative angle stay fixed.

Each substep, joints are solved by sequential impulses interleaved with contacts, then positions are corrected and the joints are checked for `break`. The impulse that breaks a joint is the larger of its linear and angular accumulators; the metadata reports each break with its frame and impulse. `collide: false` (the default) leaves the connected pair out of contact solving, so a ragdoll's neighbours or a rope's links pass through each other; `collide: true` keeps them touching.

A sleeping body joined to a moving one wakes one link per substep. A joint whose end is asleep or fixed is solved as if that end had infinite mass, so a hanging chain settles and sleeps as a whole.

### 14b. Ropes

A rope is sugar for a chain of small hidden bodies between two anchors. It is read after the bodies and the user joints, and its links add to the effect's body count. `links` controls the chain, `slack` its extra length, `thickness` its drawn width and the link radius, `density` its mass, `break` the impulse that splits it. Adjacent links never collide. A rope draws each intact joint as a line (`look: "rope"`) or a link at each body (`look: "chain"`), under or over the bodies. Ropes may hang from two bodies, one body and the world, or two world points.

### 14c. Cloth

A cloth is a grid of particles at `at`, `size` particles across and down, `cell` pixels apart. A `mask` may cut holes (a cape, a banner, a net). Its constraints are structural (right and down), shear (both diagonals), and bend (two cells apart), each scaled by `stiffness`, `shear` and `bend`; `slack` lengthens every rest length, so a flag can billow instead of staying taut. `seed` orders gust variation.

Pins hold particles to a fixed point, to `[frame, x, y]` keys (held before the first and after the last key), or to a named body's local point. Pinned particles move with their body and never react on it.

Wind is an acceleration in pixels a frame². `gust` varies it in time and across the cloth and adds a perpendicular flap, so a flag pinned along one edge ripples rather than only stretching. Gravity is the effect's. A particle that enters an obstacle or a body is pushed out; a particle struck by a moving body takes half its velocity. Cloth pushes bodies in no direction in this version: the bodies push the cloth. `tear` breaks a constraint stretched past that multiple of its rest length; the cloth then hangs from the remaining constraints and the metadata counts the tears.

Cloth is drawn as palette-shaded cells: the local edges against `light` choose a colour on the `fill` ramp, `back` colours a folded reverse face, and `outline` traces the silhouette. It is drawn under or over the bodies (`draw`), never interleaved per pixel.

### 14d. Step order

Each frame:

1. Bodies' substeps: forces, contacts (with joint-connected pairs left out), joints, integration, joint position correction, contact relaxation, restitution, breaks.
2. Fixed bodies are placed at the frame's end.
3. Cloth substeps: gravity and wind, integration, pins, constraint iterations, collisions with the world and bodies, tearing.
4. Drawing: turf, cloth under, ropes under, bodies, ropes over, cloth over.
5. Bookkeeping: metadata, paths, rest.

Cloth and bodies trade once a frame. Cloth collisions with bodies are one-way; cloth and fluid do not interact, and cloth does not enter a fluid's pressure solve.

### 14e. Limits and compatibility

Limits: at most 256 joints, 16 ropes of 2–64 links, 16 cloths and 4,096 cloth particles per effect. The source's work budget counts joint iterations, cloth particles, constraints and drawn cells. Existing sources are unchanged: no joints, ropes or cloths means no extra work and the same pixels. Cloth and joint fields are opt-in; goldens for existing bodies do not move.
